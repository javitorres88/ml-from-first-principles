Module 04 · Classification

Logistic Regression & Classification Loss

Why a threshold is not differentiable, how the logistic function fixes it, why squared error fails for probabilities, and how cross-entropy gives us log-odds we can actually interpret.

📚 Source: Lecture Notes 2 ⏱ 65 min 🧪 3 interactive labs 🐍 3 implementations

◎ Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Explain why a hard threshold function cannot be trained with gradient descent.
  • Derive the logistic (sigmoid) function, its derivative σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)), and its inverse the logit.
  • Derive binary cross-entropy from maximum likelihood under a Bernoulli model.
  • Interpret coefficients as log-odds ratios and translate them into plain language.
  • Extend to k classes with softmax and the multi-class cross-entropy loss.

1 Intuition

The problem with a hard threshold

We already have a linear model, $y = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. The obvious way to turn it into a classifier is to threshold it:

$$ \text{output} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y \ge 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y \lt 0\end{cases} $$

This works as a predictor but fails as a learner. The threshold function is flat almost everywhere — its derivative is zero — and undefined at $y=0$. Gradient descent needs a gradient: with a step function, nudging a weight slightly changes nothing until it suddenly flips a prediction. There is no direction to descend in.

What we want is a smooth, monotone function that agrees with the step function at the extremes but gives usable gradients in between, and whose output can be read as a probability.

The logistic function

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$

It maps any real number to $(0,1)$: $\sigma(-\infty)=0$, $\sigma(0)=0.5$, $\sigma(+\infty)=1$. Combined with the linear model it gives logistic regression:

$$ z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b, \qquad y = \sigma(z), \qquad \text{output} = \begin{cases}1 & y \ge 0.5\\ 0 & y \lt 0.5\end{cases} $$

Here $\sigma$ is called an activation function — the same word we will use for ReLU and tanh in module 06. Because $y\in[0,1]$ we can interpret $y$ as the estimated probability that $t=1$, and $1-y$ as the probability that $t=0$.

Cross-entropy: the right loss for probabilities

Since $y$ is a probability, we should score it as one. Cross-entropy measures the difference between two distributions — here, between our predicted $y$ and the truth $t$:

$$ L_{\text{CE}}(y,t) = \begin{cases} -\log y & t = 1\\ -\log(1-y) & t = 0\end{cases} \;=\; -t\log y - (1-t)\log(1-y) $$

The behaviour is exactly what intuition demands. If $t=1$ and we predict $y=0.99$, the loss is $-\log 0.99 \approx 0.01$. If we predict $y = 0.01$ — confidently wrong — the loss is $-\log 0.01 \approx 4.6$. The penalty for confident errors grows without bound, which is what forces the model to be honest rather than merely directionally correct.

Why not squared error?

Squared error on a sigmoid output still has a minimum in the right place, but the gradient is multiplied by $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, which is at most $0.25$ and shrinks toward zero exactly when the model is confidently wrong. Training stalls. With cross-entropy the sigmoid derivative cancels algebraically and the gradient becomes $(\hat{y}-t)\mathbf{x}$ — large when the error is large. Section 2.4 derives this.

Log-odds: the interpretation superpower

Invert the logistic function and you get the logit:

$$ \text{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p} = z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b $$

The quantity $p/(1-p)$ is the odds, and its logarithm is the log-odds (log odds ratio). This is why logistic regression remains the default in medicine, epidemiology and credit risk: each coefficient has a direct reading.

Odds ratio $e^{w_i}$MeaningExample
$\gt 1$positive association: increasing $x_i$ raises the probability of $y=1$$e^{w}=1.8$: each additional year of age multiplies the odds of the event by 1.8
$= 1$no association$w = 0$
$\lt 1$negative association$e^{w}=0.6$: a 40% reduction in odds per unit

This is a textbook example of model interpretability: how the model makes its decision is legible to a human without any post-hoc tooling. Module 16 will contrast this with SHAP, which is what you need when the model is a random forest or a neural network.

Real-world examples

Medicine · risk scores

Clinical prediction rules

Logistic regression underlies most published clinical risk scores because the coefficients are auditable: "each extra point of lactate raises the odds of mortality by 1.3×".

Finance · credit decisions

Explainable lending

Fair-lending law requires credit decisions to be explainable. A logistic scorecard with per-feature contributions satisfies that requirement in a way a deep network does not.

Epidemiology · case-control

Odds ratios

Case-control studies report odds ratios directly; logistic regression is the natural estimator, which is why the log-odds parameterisation exists at all.

NLP · the final layer

Every language model's last step

GPT outputs a vector of logits over the vocabulary, then applies softmax to get next-token probabilities. The multi-class version of this module is literally the final operation of every LLM.

Vision · CIFAR-10

Ten-way classification

A linear model with $10\times3072$ weights plus softmax is the baseline every image classifier must beat — and the "template" interpretation of its rows explains what a linear model can and cannot see.

Public health · screening

Probability, not just a label

Because logistic regression outputs calibrated probabilities, thresholds can be chosen per-deployment to trade sensitivity against specificity (module 02, lab 3) rather than baked in at 0.5.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Properties of the logistic function

Derivation — the derivative and the inverse
  1. Write $\sigma(z) = (1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ and differentiate with the chain rule: $$ \sigma'(z) = -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z}) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}. $$
  2. Rewrite in terms of $\sigma$ itself. Since $e^{-z} = \frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}$ (from $\sigma = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$) and $1+e^{-z} = \frac{1}{\sigma}$: $$ \sigma'(z) = \frac{(1-\sigma)/\sigma}{1/\sigma^2} = \sigma(1-\sigma). $$ The derivative is maximal at $z=0$, where $\sigma = 1/2$ and $\sigma' = 1/4$. That single fact explains both the vanishing-gradient problem in deep sigmoid networks and the bias-correction step in Adam (module 07).
  3. Invert: solve $p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ for $z$. $$ \frac{1}{p} = 1+e^{-z} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{-z} = \frac{1-p}{p} \;\Rightarrow\; z = \log\frac{p}{1-p} = \text{logit}(p). $$
  4. ∎ So $\sigma$ and the logit are inverse functions: the linear model lives in log-odds space, and $\sigma$ converts log-odds to probability.

2.2 Cross-entropy from maximum likelihood

Derivation — the Bernoulli likelihood gives cross-entropy
  1. Model the label as a Bernoulli draw with success probability $\hat{y} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b)$: $$ p(t\mid\mathbf{x}) = \hat{y}^{\,t}(1-\hat{y})^{\,1-t}, \qquad t\in\{0,1\}. $$ Check: $t=1$ gives $\hat{y}$, $t=0$ gives $1-\hat{y}$. One expression, both cases.
  2. Over $N$ independent samples the likelihood is a product; take the log: $$ \log L(\mathbf{w},b) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[t_i\log\hat{y}_i + (1-t_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\Big]. $$
  3. Maximising the log-likelihood = minimising its negative, averaged: $$ J(\mathbf{w},b) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[t_i\log\hat{y}_i + (1-t_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\Big] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i L_{\text{CE}}(\hat{y}_i, t_i). $$
  4. ∎ Cross-entropy is not an arbitrary choice; it is the negative log-likelihood of the model we assumed. Equivalently, it is the KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(t\,\|\,\hat{y})$ plus the entropy of $t$ — and the entropy term does not depend on the parameters, so minimising cross-entropy minimises the divergence between the true and predicted label distributions.

2.3 The gradient is (prediction − truth) × input

Derivation — the sigmoid derivative cancels
  1. For one sample, with $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$ and $\hat{y}=\sigma(z)$, the loss is $L = -t\log\hat{y} - (1-t)\log(1-\hat{y})$.
  2. Differentiate with respect to $z$ using the chain rule: $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = -\frac{t}{\hat{y}} + \frac{1-t}{1-\hat{y}} = \frac{\hat{y}-t}{\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})}. $$
  3. Multiply by $\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} = \hat{y}(1-\hat{y})$: $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\hat{y}-t}{\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})}\cdot\hat{y}(1-\hat{y}) = \hat{y} - t. $$ The awkward $\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})$ cancels exactly. This is the entire reason cross-entropy is preferred to squared error on a sigmoid output.
  4. Propagate to the parameters with $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j} = x_j$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1$: $$ \boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = (\hat{y}-t)\,\mathbf{x}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \hat{y}-t\;} $$
  5. ∎ The gradient is zero only when the prediction equals the label, and its magnitude grows with the error — no saturation. Vectorised over the dataset: $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J = \frac{1}{N}X^\top(\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{t})$.

2.4 Convexity

The Hessian of the logistic cost with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is

$$ H = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top = \frac{1}{N}X^\top S X, \qquad S = \mathrm{diag}\big(\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)\big) $$

Since $\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i) \gt 0$ and $S$ is diagonal positive, $H$ is positive semi-definite: $\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = \frac1N\sum_i \hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)(\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{x}_i)^2 \ge 0$. The logistic cost is therefore convex — gradient descent converges to the global optimum, and there are no local minima to escape. (Note the difference from a neural network, where this convexity is lost the moment a hidden layer is added.)

2.5 Reading coefficients as odds ratios

Increase feature $x_j$ by one unit, holding everything else fixed. Then $z$ increases by $w_j$ and the odds are multiplied by $e^{w_j}$:

$$ \frac{\text{odds}(x_j+1)}{\text{odds}(x_j)} = \frac{e^{z + w_j}}{e^{z}} = e^{w_j} $$

So $w_j$ is the additive change in log-odds and $e^{w_j}$ is the multiplicative change in odds. Because the model is linear in log-odds, the effect of a feature does not depend on the values of the other features — a strong assumption (no interaction) and a frequent source of misinterpretation. Always state the units and the reference range.

2.6 From two classes to $k$ classes

With $d$ inputs and $k \ge 3$ classes we need $k\times d$ weights arranged as a matrix $W$ and a $k$-vector of biases $\mathbf{b}$:

$$ \mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}, \qquad y_k = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{k'} e^{z_{k'}}} $$

Each $y_k\in(0,1)$ and $\sum_k y_k = 1$, so the output is a probability distribution over classes. The loss is the multi-class cross-entropy

$$ L_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} t_k\log y_k = -\mathbf{t}^\top\log\mathbf{y} $$

with the logarithm applied element-wise, and $\mathbf{t}$ a one-hot vector.

Derivation — the softmax gradient is again (prediction − truth)
  1. Differentiate the softmax: for $j = k$, $\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial z_k} = y_k(1-y_k)$; for $j\ne k$, $\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial z_j} = -y_ky_j$. In matrix form this is the Jacobian $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{y}) - \mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^\top$.
  2. For a one-hot target with true class $c$, $L = -\log y_c$, so $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = -\frac{1}{y_c}\frac{\partial y_c}{\partial z_j} = -\frac{1}{y_c}\big(\mathbb{1}[j=c]\,y_c - y_cy_j\big) = y_j - \mathbb{1}[j=c]. $$
  3. In vector form: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{t}$. ∎
  4. Hence $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{t})\mathbf{x}^\top$ — structurally identical to the binary case. This is why the same code path handles 2 or 200 classes.
A useful invariant

Softmax is invariant to adding a constant to all logits: $\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}+c) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$. In code you exploit this by subtracting the maximum logit before exponentiating, which prevents overflow: e = np.exp(z - z.max()); p = e / e.sum(). The temperature in lab 3 is exactly this idea in reverse — dividing logits by $T \gt 1$ flattens the distribution, $T \lt 1$ sharpens it.

3 Interactive visualizations

Lab 1 · the activation

Inside the sigmoid

Drag the point along the curve. Three numbers deserve attention. At $z=0$ the probability is exactly $0.5$ and the slope $\sigma'(z)$ is maximal at $0.25$. By $z=4$ the slope has collapsed to about $0.018$ — a gradient nearly $14\times$ smaller — which is the vanishing-gradient problem in miniature.

Watch the odds readout: at $z=0$ odds are 1:1; at $z=2.2$ they are 9:1. Log-odds are linear in $z$, probabilities are not.

Lab 2 · training

Fitting the boundary by gradient descent

Two overlapping classes, one linear boundary, learned by gradient descent on the cross-entropy. Press Run and watch the boundary rotate into place while the shading becomes more confident. The loss is convex, so the only thing the learning rate controls is how fast you arrive — try $0.05$ versus $2.5$.

Notice that the boundary never fully separates the classes: they overlap by construction, and the model settles on the line that maximises the likelihood of the observed labels. Also notice that the shading is smooth near the boundary and saturates far from it — that is $\sigma$ doing its job.

Lab 3 · multi-class

Softmax, temperature and cross-entropy

Three logits in, three probabilities out. Raise the true class's logit and watch its probability approach 1 while the loss falls toward 0. Now push a wrong class's logit up: the loss climbs steeply — confident errors are expensive, exactly as the $ -\log p$ curve says.

Finally sweep the temperature. $T \to 4$ flattens the distribution toward uniform; $T \to 0.25$ sharpens it into a near one-hot. Every language model you have used exposes this same knob.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 04 — logistic regression from scratch: sigmoid, cross-entropy, GD."""
import math


def sigmoid(z):
    # numerically stable: never exponentiate a large positive number
    if z >= 0:
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-z))
    e = math.exp(z)
    return e / (1.0 + e)


def logit(p):
    return math.log(p / (1.0 - p))


def bce(y_hat, t):
    """Binary cross-entropy for one sample (clipped to avoid log(0))."""
    eps = 1e-12
    y_hat = min(max(y_hat, eps), 1 - eps)
    return -(t * math.log(y_hat) + (1 - t) * math.log(1 - y_hat))


def predict_proba(X, w, b):
    return [sigmoid(sum(wj * xj for wj, xj in zip(w, x)) + b) for x in X]


def fit_logistic(X, t, lr=0.5, epochs=500, l2=0.0):
    """Full-batch gradient descent. Gradient is (y_hat - t) * x."""
    n, m = len(X), len(X[0])
    w, b = [0.0] * m, 0.0
    for _ in range(epochs):
        p = predict_proba(X, w, b)
        err = [pi - ti for pi, ti in zip(p, t)]
        gw = [sum(err[i] * X[i][j] for i in range(n)) / n + l2 * w[j] for j in range(m)]
        gb = sum(err) / n
        w = [wj - lr * g for wj, g in zip(w, gw)]
        b -= lr * gb
    return w, b


def accuracy(X, t, w, b):
    p = predict_proba(X, w, b)
    return sum((pi >= 0.5) == ti for pi, ti in zip(p, t)) / len(t)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # y = 1 when x1 + x2 > 0 (separable, so the fit should be confident)
    X = [[2, 1], [1, 2], [2, 2], [-1, -1], [-2, -1], [-1, -2]]
    t = [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0]
    w, b = fit_logistic(X, t, lr=0.5, epochs=2000)
    print("w =", [round(v, 2) for v in w], "b =", round(b, 2))     # roughly [2.4, 2.4], 0
    print("accuracy =", accuracy(X, t, w, b))                       # 1.0
    print("odds ratio per unit of x1 =", round(math.exp(w[0]), 2))

"""Module 04 — vectorised logistic regression + stable softmax."""
import numpy as np


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def softmax(Z):
    """Rows are samples. Subtracting the row max keeps exp() in range."""
    Z = np.atleast_2d(Z)
    Z = Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    E = np.exp(Z)
    return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


def cross_entropy(P, T):
    """Mean cross-entropy for binary (1-D P) or multi-class (2-D P) predictions."""
    P = np.clip(np.asarray(P, float), 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)
    T = np.asarray(T, float)
    if P.ndim == 1:
        return float(-np.mean(T * np.log(P) + (1 - T) * np.log(1 - P)))
    return float(-np.mean(np.sum(T * np.log(P), axis=1)))


def fit_logistic(X, t, lr=0.5, epochs=2000, l2=0.0):
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    t = np.asarray(t, float)
    n, m = X.shape
    w, b = np.zeros(m), 0.0
    losses = []
    for _ in range(epochs):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w + b)
        err = p - t
        w -= lr * (X.T @ err / n + l2 * w)
        b -= lr * err.mean()
        losses.append(cross_entropy(p, t))
    return w, b, losses


def fit_softmax(X, T, lr=0.5, epochs=2000):
    """Multi-class logistic regression; T is one-hot (n, k)."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    T = np.asarray(T, float)
    n, m = X.shape
    k = T.shape[1]
    W, b = np.zeros((m, k)), np.zeros(k)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        Y = softmax(X @ W + b)
        G = (Y - T) / n                      # the whole gradient, one line
        W -= lr * (X.T @ G)
        b -= lr * G.sum(axis=0)
    return W, b


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    X = np.vstack([rng.normal(-1, 1, (100, 2)), rng.normal(1, 1, (100, 2))])
    t = np.array([0] * 100 + [1] * 100)
    w, b, losses = fit_logistic(X, t, lr=0.5)
    print("loss", round(losses[0], 3), "->", round(losses[-1], 3))
    print("accuracy", ((sigmoid(X @ w + b) >= 0.5) == t).mean())
    print("softmax demo:", np.round(softmax([[1.0, 2.0, 3.0]]), 3))

"""Module 04 — scikit-learn logistic regression, odds ratios, and calibration."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
names = load_breast_cancer().feature_names
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=0)

clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=1.0))
clf.fit(X_tr, y_tr)

# Cross-validated AUC (a threshold-free view of the ranking quality)
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
print("CV AUC:", np.round(cross_val_score(clf, X_tr, y_tr, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc").mean(), 3))

prob = clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
print("test AUC:", round(roc_auc_score(y_te, prob), 3))
print(classification_report(y_te, clf.predict(X_te), digits=3))

# Odds ratios: with standardised features, exp(w) is the odds multiplier per 1 SD.
w = clf[-1].coef_[0]
odds = np.exp(w)
order = np.argsort(-np.abs(w))[:6]
print("\nfeature                 coef    odds ratio")
for i in order:
    print(f"{names[i]:22s} {w[i]:6.3f}   {odds[i]:8.3f}")

# The threshold is a deployment decision, not a property of the model.
for thr in (0.2, 0.5, 0.8):
    pred = (prob >= thr).astype(int)
    tp = ((pred == 1) & (y_te == 1)).sum()
    fn = ((pred == 0) & (y_te == 1)).sum()
    fp = ((pred == 1) & (y_te == 0)).sum()
    tn = ((pred == 0) & (y_te == 0)).sum()
    print(f"thr={thr}: sens={tp/(tp+fn):.3f} spec={tn/(tn+fp):.3f} prec={tp/max(tp+fp,1):.3f}")
The NumPy block contains the single most reused line in deep learning: G = (Y - T) / n. Whether the output is a sigmoid or a softmax, with cross-entropy the gradient of the logits is prediction minus truth.

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

Why can't we train a linear classifier with a hard threshold using gradient descent?

A step function gives no gradient signal: perturbing a weight changes nothing until a prediction flips. The sigmoid is a smooth surrogate with a usable derivative.

Q2

For $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ and $\hat{y}=\sigma(z)$, the derivative $\partial L_{\text{CE}}/\partial z$ equals:

The factor $\hat y(1-\hat y)$ from the sigmoid derivative cancels with the $1/(\hat y(1-\hat y))$ from the log. Answer B is what you get with squared error — and it saturates.

Q3

A fitted coefficient is $w_j = 0.7$ for a feature measured in years. How should this be reported?

Logistic regression is linear in log-odds, so $w_j$ is an additive change in log-odds and $e^{w_j}$ a multiplicative change in odds. The change in probability depends on the baseline risk.

Q4

Why subtract the maximum logit before exponentiating in softmax?

$\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}+c) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$. Subtracting the max guarantees the largest exponent is $e^0=1$, so no overflow, with identical probabilities.

Q5

The logistic loss is convex in $\mathbf{w}$ because:

Positive semi-definite Hessian ⟺ convex ⟺ any local minimum is global. This property is lost as soon as a hidden layer is inserted, which is the subject of module 07.

Term
Logit
Definition
$\log\frac{p}{1-p}$, the inverse of the sigmoid. Logistic regression is a linear model in logit space.
Term
Odds ratio
Definition
$e^{w_j}$: the factor by which the odds of $y=1$ change per one-unit increase in $x_j$. $\gt 1$ positive association, $\lt 1$ negative.
Term
Cross-entropy
Definition
$-t\log\hat y - (1-t)\log(1-\hat y)$: the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli model. Unbounded penalty for confident errors.
Term
Activation function
Definition
A fixed non-linear function applied to a linear score. In logistic regression it is $\sigma$; in hidden layers it is usually ReLU.
Term
Softmax
Definition
$e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$: maps $k$ logits to a probability distribution. Shift-invariant, so code subtracts the max.
Term
Why not MSE on a sigmoid?
Definition
The gradient is multiplied by $\sigma'(z)\le 0.25$, which shrinks exactly when the model is confidently wrong, so training stalls. Cross-entropy cancels that factor.
Term
Calibration
Definition
A model is calibrated if, among samples it scores at $p$, roughly a fraction $p$ are positive. Logistic regression is often reasonably calibrated out of the box; deep nets usually are not.
Term
Decision threshold
Definition
The probability above which you predict 1. It is a deployment choice, not a model parameter: 0.5 minimises error under equal costs, but screening usually wants a lower threshold.

Practice problems

Easy

Logit arithmetic

For $\mathbf{w} = (1.5, -0.5)$, $b = -0.2$, and $\mathbf{x} = (2, 4)$: compute $z$, the predicted probability, and the odds. Then find the change in $x_1$ needed to double the odds.

$z = 1.5(2) - 0.5(4) - 0.2 = 3 - 2 - 0.2 = 0.8$.

$\hat y = \sigma(0.8) = 1/(1+e^{-0.8}) = 0.690$. Odds $= 0.690/0.310 = 2.226$.

Doubling the odds means adding $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ to the log-odds, i.e. $\Delta x_1 = 0.693/1.5 = 0.462$.

Medium

Show that squared error saturates

Let $L_{\text{MSE}} = \frac12(\hat y - t)^2$ with $\hat y = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b)$. Derive $\partial L_{\text{MSE}}/\partial \mathbf{w}$ and explain why it vanishes for confident errors. Compare with the cross-entropy gradient at $\hat y = 0.01$, $t=1$.

  1. $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{w} = (\hat y - t)\,\sigma'(z)\,\mathbf{x} = (\hat y - t)\,\hat y(1-\hat y)\,\mathbf{x}$.
  2. At $\hat y = 0.01$, $t=1$: $(0.01-1)\cdot 0.01 \cdot 0.99 = -0.0098$. The gradient magnitude is below $0.01$ — essentially no signal, even though the prediction is maximally wrong.
  3. Cross-entropy at the same point: $\hat y - t = -0.99$ — a gradient two orders of magnitude larger. ∎
  4. Squared error is not wrong here (the minimum is the same), it is merely badly conditioned: the region where the model needs the most correction is the region where it learns slowest.
Hard

Cross-entropy, entropy and KL divergence

Show that for a true distribution $q$ and model distribution $p$, minimising the cross-entropy $H(q,p) = -\sum_k q_k\log p_k$ over $p$ is equivalent to minimising $D_{\text{KL}}(q\|p)$. When does the cross-entropy equal the entropy?

  1. By definition $D_{\text{KL}}(q\|p) = \sum_k q_k\log\frac{q_k}{p_k} = \sum_k q_k\log q_k - \sum_k q_k\log p_k$.
  2. Recognise the two terms: $\sum_k q_k\log q_k = -H(q)$ (negative entropy) and $\sum_k q_k\log p_k = -H(q,p)$.
  3. So $D_{\text{KL}}(q\|p) = H(q,p) - H(q)$. Since $H(q)$ does not depend on the model, minimising $H(q,p)$ over $p$ minimises $D_{\text{KL}}$. ∎
  4. Cross-entropy equals entropy exactly when $p = q$ (KL $=0$). For one-hot targets, $H(q) = 0$, so the cross-entropy is the KL divergence itself — and its minimum value is 0.
  5. Practical consequence: cross-entropy loss is not comparable across datasets with different label distributions, because its zero point depends on $H(q)$. Comparing losses across models on the same dataset is fine; across datasets it is not.

A clinical risk score with odds ratios you can defend

Build a binary classifier whose coefficients you can explain to a clinician, and quantify the difference between a model that ranks well and a model whose probabilities you can trust.

Brief
