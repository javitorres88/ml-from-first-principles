Module 15 · Sequential decisions

Reinforcement Learning

Markov decision processes, policies and returns; the Bellman equations; value and policy iteration; then model-free TD learning, Q-learning, ε-greedy exploration and function approximation.

📚 Source: Lecture Notes 13 ⏱ 80 min 🧪 2 interactive labs 🐍 3 implementations

◎ Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Formalise a problem as an MDP (S, A, T, R, γ) and compute the optimal policy by hand for a grid world.
  • Derive the Bellman optimality equations for V* and Q*.
  • Implement value iteration and policy iteration and compare their convergence.
  • Derive the TD and Q-learning updates and explain off-policy learning.
  • Balance exploration and exploitation with ε-greedy and decaying schedules.

1 Intuition

Learning from reward instead of labels

Supervised learning needs a teacher who knows the right answer for every input. Reinforcement learning (RL) replaces the teacher with a scalar reward delivered after acting. The agent must discover, by trial and error, which actions lead to good outcomes — the way you learn to walk, drive or play a sport.

The formal setting is a Markov decision process (MDP), defined by:

ElementMeaning
States $s\in S$everything the agent needs to know about the world
Actions $a\in A$ (or $A(s)$)what the agent can do
Transition model $T(s,a,s') = \Pr(s'\mid s,a)$the dynamics — possibly stochastic
Reward $R(s,a,s')$the immediate payoff, sometimes written $R(s)$ or $R(s')$
Start state, terminal stateswhere episodes begin and end

"Markov" means the future is independent of the past given the present: action outcomes depend only on the current state. That assumption is what makes the whole formalism tractable — the state is a sufficient statistic for everything that matters.

The running example is a grid world: an agent moves in a maze, walls block it, movement is noisy (80% of the time an action succeeds, 10% each it slips sideways), there is a small living reward each step and a large terminal reward. The goal is to maximise the sum of rewards — the utility — over time.

Policies and values

A policy $\pi: S\to A$ specifies an action for every state. The optimal policy $\pi^\*$ maximises expected utility, and which policy is optimal depends on the transition probabilities and the rewards — change the living reward and the agent's willingness to dawdle changes with it.

Two quantities capture optimality. The value of a state is the expected discounted return from it:

$$ V^\*(s) = \max_{a}\;\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\*(s')\Big] $$

and the Q-value is the same quantity after committing to a specific first action:

$$ Q^\*(s,a) = \sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma\max_{a'}Q^\*(s',a')\Big] $$

The discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$ makes distant rewards count less, keeps the sum finite for infinite horizons, and encodes impatience. These two equations are the Bellman equations: a one-step lookahead relationship that characterises the optimal values, and the foundation of everything that follows.

Two ways to solve a known MDP

MethodProcedureCharacter
Value iterationinitialise $V=0$; repeatedly apply the Bellman update to every state until $V$ converges; then read off $\pi^\*(s)=\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$simple, each sweep is one Bellman backup
Policy iterationstart with a policy; evaluate its values; improve it greedily; repeat until the policy stops changingusually converges in fewer iterations, each one more expensive

Both require knowing $T$ and $R$. Reinforcement learning is the case where they are unknown: you must act to find out. That introduces the tension at the heart of the field.

Exploration versus exploitation

  • Exploration — try unknown actions to gather information.
  • Exploitation — use what you already know to collect reward.
  • Regret — the total cost of the mistakes you inevitably make while learning.
  • Sampling — you may need to repeat state–action pairs many times to get reliable estimates.
  • Generalisation — what you learn in one state may apply to others, which is how RL scales.
Why pure exploitation fails

If you always take the best action according to your current (wrong) estimates, you never discover the better option you have not tried. You get stuck in a local optimum of your own ignorance. $\epsilon$-greedy exploration is the simplest fix: with small probability $\epsilon$ act randomly, otherwise act greedily. Its properties are exactly as advertised — every state–action pair is tried infinitely often (so estimates converge), but it keeps doing stupid things forever, so $\epsilon$ is usually decayed toward zero over time.

Model-based, model-free, and the two model-free families

ApproachWhat is learnedExamples
Model-basedestimate $T$ and $R$, then solve the MDPDyna, planning with a learned model
Model-free, value-basedestimate $V$ or $Q$ directly from experienceTD learning, Q-learning, DQN
Model-free, policy-basedlearn the policy directlypolicy gradients, REINFORCE, PPO

This module focuses on the value-based family: temporal difference (TD) learning for prediction and Q-learning for control.

TD learning and Q-learning

TD learning replaces the expectation in the Bellman equation with a single sampled transition — the "update toward a one-step lookahead" idea:

$$ V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma V(s')}_{\text{TD target}} - \underbrace{V(s)}_{\text{current estimate}}\Big] $$

The bracket is the TD error. Averaged over samples it converges to the true value (with a decaying learning rate), and it is online: you can update after every single step rather than waiting for an episode to finish.

TD learning evaluates a policy but cannot improve it, because improving requires knowing the transition model. Q-learning sidesteps this by learning the action-value function directly:

$$ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\Big] $$

The $\max$ over next actions is what makes this off-policy: the update uses the greedy continuation even if the agent actually behaved randomly. That is why Q-learning converges to the optimal policy even when the samples come from a suboptimal exploring policy — a remarkable property, with two caveats: every state–action pair must be visited infinitely often, and the learning rate must satisfy

$$ \sum_t \alpha(t) = \infty, \qquad \sum_t \alpha^2(t) \lt \infty $$

satisfied for instance by $\alpha(t) = 1/t$ or (better, numerically) $\alpha(t) = K/(K+t)$.

Generalising across states

A Q-table needs one entry per state–action pair. For chess that is $10^{43}$ states; for a robot with continuous sensors it is uncountable. The fix is function approximation: represent $Q$ with parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$,

$$ Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s,a) = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{f}(s,a) = w_1f_1(s,a) + \dots + w_nf_n(s,a) $$

where the features $f_i$ describe the state (distance to the nearest ghost, distance to the nearest dot, whether Pac-Man is in a tunnel). The update becomes a gradient step on the TD error:

$$ \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \alpha\Big[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s',a') - Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s,a)\Big]\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s,a) $$

With the wrong features the best possible approximation can be terrible — but with good features the compression is extraordinary: chess value functions have been approximated with about 30 weights and still play decently. Deep Q-networks (DQN) replace the linear form with a CNN over the last four frames and learned weights, and learned $Q$ for 49 Atari games from pixels.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Return, discounting, and why $\gamma \lt 1$

For a trajectory $s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, a_1, r_2, \dots$ the discounted return is

$$ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1} $$
Derivation — the recursive form and the boundedness condition
  1. Factor out the first reward: $$ G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\big(r_{t+2} + \gamma r_{t+3} + \dots\big) = r_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}. $$ This recursion is what makes Bellman equations possible: the return from now equals the immediate reward plus the discounted return from next step.
  2. If rewards are bounded, $|r|\le R_{\max}$, then $|G_t| \le R_{\max}\sum_k\gamma^k = \frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$, which is finite iff $\gamma \lt 1$. ∎
  3. $\gamma$ also has an interpretation as a horizon: $1/(1-\gamma)$ is the "effective number of future steps" being optimised. $\gamma=0.9$ looks about 10 steps ahead; $\gamma=0.99$ about 100.

2.2 The Bellman optimality equations

Derivation — from the definition of optimal value
  1. By definition, $V^\*(s) = \max_\pi \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s]$.
  2. Use the return recursion and split the expectation over the first action and transition: $$ V^\*(s) = \max_{a}\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid s_{t+1}=s']\big]. $$
  3. The inner expectation, under an optimal continuation, is $V^\*(s')$. Hence $$ V^\*(s) = \max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\big[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V^\*(s')\big]. $$ ∎
  4. The same argument one step later gives the $Q$ form. The two are related by $V^\*(s) = \max_a Q^\*(s,a)$ and $Q^\*(s,a) = \sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')[R + \gamma\max_{a'}Q^\*(s',a')]$.
  5. Existence and uniqueness: the Bellman operator $\mathcal{B}$ defined by the right-hand side is a contraction in the sup norm with modulus $\gamma \lt 1$. By the Banach fixed-point theorem it has a unique fixed point, which is $V^\*$ — and iterating $\mathcal{B}$ from any starting point converges to it geometrically. That is precisely why value iteration works.

2.3 Value iteration converges

Value iteration applies the Bellman operator repeatedly: $V_{k+1} = \mathcal{B}V_k$.

Proof — the contraction argument and the error bound
  1. For any two value functions $U, W$: $$ \|\mathcal{B}U - \mathcal{B}W\|_\infty = \max_s\Big|\max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\big[R+\gamma U(s')\big] - \max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\big[R+\gamma W(s')\big]\Big|. $$
  2. Using $|\max_a f(a) - \max_a g(a)| \le \max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$ and $\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')=1$: $$ \le \gamma\max_s\max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')\big|U(s')-W(s')\big| \le \gamma\|U-W\|_\infty. $$
  3. So $\mathcal{B}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction; iterating gives $\|V_k - V^\*\|\le\gamma^k\|V_0-V^\*\|$, geometric convergence. ∎
  4. A practical stopping rule follows: after a sweep with maximum change $\Delta$, the current policy is within $\frac{\gamma\Delta}{1-\gamma}$ of optimal in value. Lab 1 reports $\Delta$ so you can watch it collapse.

2.4 Why Q-learning is off-policy

Write the Q-learning update as a stochastic approximation to the Bellman optimality operator:

$$ Q_{t+1}(s,a) = Q_t(s,a) + \alpha_t(s,a)\Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q_t(s',a')}_{\text{sample of }(\mathcal{B}Q_t)(s,a)} - Q_t(s,a)\Big] $$

The target uses $\max_{a'}$ regardless of which action the behaviour policy actually took next. So the update is estimating $Q^\*$ — the values of the greedy policy — while the data comes from an exploratory policy. That decoupling is what "off-policy" means, and it is why Q-learning can learn the optimal policy from random exploration.

Convergence conditions

Watkins and Dayan proved convergence to $Q^\*$ with probability 1 provided (i) every state–action pair is visited infinitely often, and (ii) the learning rates satisfy $\sum_t\alpha_t=\infty$ and $\sum_t\alpha_t^2\lt\infty$. Condition (ii) is why a constant learning rate is technically incorrect but practically common: it tracks a moving target in non-stationary problems.

2.5 Function approximation and the deadly triad

With linear function approximation $Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s,a) = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{f}(s,a)$, the semi-gradient TD update is

$$ \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \alpha\big[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s',a') - Q_{\mathbf{w}}(s,a)\big]\mathbf{f}(s,a) $$

Note the target depends on $\mathbf{w}$ too, so this is not true gradient descent — hence "semi-gradient". The combination of three ingredients — function approximation, bootstrapping (using an estimate as a target) and off-policy data — is called the deadly triad, and it can make training diverge. DQN's two tricks address it directly: an experience replay buffer (breaks temporal correlation in the samples) and a target network (a slowly updated copy used to compute targets, so the target stops chasing itself).

2.6 Regret and exploration schedules

Regret measures the total reward lost relative to acting optimally from the start:

$$ \text{Regret}(T) = T\cdot V^\*(s_0) - \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t $$

Minimising regret is a stronger requirement than learning the optimal policy — it requires learning efficiently. Fixed $\epsilon$-greedy has linear regret (it never stops exploring); decaying $\epsilon$ achieves logarithmic regret in many settings, and optimistic initialisation or explicit exploration bonuses (count-based, curiosity-driven) do better still.

Q-learning without a model

Same grid, but the agent is not told the dynamics — it learns $Q(s,a)$ from sampled transitions. Values start at zero and fill in from the states the agent actually visits.

Run a few hundred episodes with $\epsilon = 0.3$. The best-return readout climbs and the policy arrows point at the goal. 4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 15 — value iteration, policy iteration, TD learning and Q-learning."""
import random

ACTIONS = [(-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)]


class GridWorld:
    def __init__(self, size=5, goal=(0, 4), pit=(2, 2), living=-0.04, gamma=0.9, slip=0.1):
        self.n, self.goal, self.pit = size, goal, pit
        self.living, self.gamma, self.slip = living, gamma, slip
        self.walls = {(1, 1), (3, 3)}

    def is_wall(self, r, c):
        return not (0 <= r < self.n and 0 <= c < self.n) or (r, c) in self.walls

    def step(self, state, action):
        """Returns (next_state, reward, done). Movement is stochastic."""
        r, c = state
        if random.random() < self.slip:
            action = random.randrange(4)
        dr, dc = ACTIONS[action]
        nr, nc = r + dr, c + dc
        if self.is_wall(nr, nc):
            nr, nc = r, c
        reward = self.living
        if (nr, nc) == self.goal:
            reward += 10
        if (nr, nc) == self.pit:
            reward -= 10
        done = (nr, nc) in (self.goal, self.pit)
        return (nr, nc), reward, done

    def states(self):
        return [(r, c) for r in range(self.n) for c in range(self.n) if not self.is_wall(r, c)]


def value_iteration(env, iters=200, tol=1e-6):
    V = {s: 0.0 for s in env.states()}
    for it in range(iters):
        delta = 0.0
        new = dict(V)
        for s in env.states():
            if s in (env.goal, env.pit):
                continue
            best = max(
                env.living + (10 if env.step(s, a)[0] == env.goal else -10 if env.step(s, a)[0] == env.pit else 0)
                + env.gamma * V[env.step(s, a)[0]]
                for a in range(4)
            )
            delta = max(delta, abs(best - V[s]))
            new[s] = best
        V = new
        if delta < tol:
            break
    policy = {}
    for s in env.states():
        if s in (env.goal, env.pit):
            continue
        policy[s] = max(range(4), key=lambda a: V[env.step(s, a)[0]])
    return V, policy, it + 1


def td_evaluate(env, policy, episodes=500, alpha=0.1):
    """TD(0) policy evaluation with a running average."""
    V = {s: 0.0 for s in env.states()}
    for _ in range(episodes):
        s = (env.n - 1, 0)
        for _ in range(100):
            a = policy[s]
            s2, r, done = env.step(s, a)
            V[s] += alpha * (r + env.gamma * V[s2] - V[s])
            s = s2
            if done:
                break
    return V


def q_learning(env, episodes=2000, alpha=0.2, gamma=0.9, eps=0.2, eps_decay=0.999):
    Q = {s: [0.0] * 4 for s in env.states()}
    returns = []
    for ep in range(episodes):
        s = (env.n - 1, 0)
        total = 0.0
        for _ in range(100):
            a = random.randrange(4) if random.random() < eps else max(range(4), key=lambda i: Q[s][i])
            s2, r, done = env.step(s, a)
            target = r + (0 if done else gamma * max(Q[s2]))
            Q[s][a] += alpha * (target - Q[s][a])
            total += r
            s = s2
            if done:
                break
        returns.append(total)
        eps *= eps_decay
    policy = {s: max(range(4), key=lambda i: Q[s][i]) for s in env.states()}
    return Q, policy, returns


if __name__ == "__main__":
    random.seed(0)
    env = GridWorld()
    V, policy, iters = value_iteration(env)
    print("value iteration converged in", iters, "sweeps")
    print("V(start) =", round(V[(env.n - 1, 0)], 3))

    Q, q_policy, returns = q_learning(env, episodes=1500)
    print("mean return, first 100 episodes:", round(sum(returns[:100]) / 100, 2))
    print("mean return, last 100 episodes :", round(sum(returns[-100:]) / 100, 2))
    agree = sum(1 for s in policy if policy[s] == q_policy[s]) / len(policy)
    print("policy agreement with value iteration:", round(agree, 3))

"""Module 15 — vectorised value iteration, n-step returns, and SARSA vs. Q-learning."""
import numpy as np


class GridMDP:
    """Fully specified MDP: T[s,a,s'] and R[s,a]. Enables exact comparison."""

    def __init__(self, n=5, goal=(0, 4), pit=(2, 2), gamma=0.9, living=-0.04, slip=0.1):
        self.n, self.goal, self.pit, self.gamma = n, goal, pit, gamma
        self.living, self.slip = living, slip
        self.walls = {(1, 1), (3, 3)}
        self.states = [(r, c) for r in range(n) for c in range(n) if (r, c) not in self.walls]
        self.index = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(self.states)}
        self.T, self.R = self._build()

    def _move(self, s, a):
        dr, dc = [(-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)][a]
        r, c = s[0] + dr, s[1] + dc
        if not (0 <= r < self.n and 0 <= c < self.n) or (r, c) in self.walls:
            return s
        return (r, c)

    def _build(self):
        S, A = len(self.states), 4
        T = np.zeros((S, A, S))
        R = np.zeros((S, A))
        for i, s in enumerate(self.states):
            for a in range(A):
                outcomes = [(self._move(s, a), 1 - self.slip)]
                for b in range(A):
                    if b != a:
                        outcomes.append((self._move(s, b), self.slip / 3))
                for s2, p in outcomes:
                    T[i, a, self.index[s2]] += p
                    r = self.living + (10 if s2 == self.goal else -10 if s2 == self.pit else 0)
                    R[i, a] += p * r
        return T, R

    def terminal(self):
        mask = np.zeros(len(self.states), bool)
        for t in (self.goal, self.pit):
            if t in self.index:
                mask[self.index[t]] = True
        return mask


def value_iteration(mdp, tol=1e-8):
    V = np.zeros(len(mdp.states))
    term = mdp.terminal()
    for it in range(1000):
        Q = mdp.R + mdp.gamma * mdp.T @ V
        Vnew = np.where(term, 0.0, Q.max(1))
        delta = np.abs(Vnew - V).max()
        V = Vnew
        if delta < tol:
            break
    return V, Q.argmax(1), it + 1


def policy_iteration(mdp):
    pi = np.zeros(len(mdp.states), int)
    for it in range(100):
        # policy evaluation (exact linear solve)
        P = mdp.T[np.arange(len(mdp.states)), pi]
        R = mdp.R[np.arange(len(mdp.states)), pi]
        V = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(mdp.states)) - mdp.gamma * P, R)
        Q = mdp.R + mdp.gamma * mdp.T @ V
        new_pi = Q.argmax(1)
        if np.array_equal(new_pi, pi):
            return V, pi, it + 1
        pi = new_pi
    return V, pi, 100


def q_learning_tabular(mdp, episodes=2000, alpha=0.2, eps=0.2, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Q = np.zeros((len(mdp.states), 4))
    start = mdp.index[(mdp.n - 1, 0)]
    term = mdp.terminal()
    returns = []
    for _ in range(episodes):
        s, total = start, 0.0
        for _ in range(100):
            a = rng.integers(4) if rng.random() < eps else int(Q[s].argmax())
            probs = mdp.T[s, a]
            s2 = int(rng.choice(len(probs), p=probs / probs.sum()))
            r = mdp.R[s, a]
            target = r + (0 if term[s2] else mdp.gamma * Q[s2].max())
            Q[s, a] += alpha * (target - Q[s, a])
            total += r
            s = s2
            if term[s]:
                break
        returns.append(total)
    return Q, returns


if __name__ == "__main__":
    mdp = GridMDP()
    V, pi, it_v = value_iteration(mdp)
    Vp, pip, it_p = policy_iteration(mdp)
    print(f"value iteration: {it_v} sweeps, policy iteration: {it_p} rounds")
    print("policies agree:", bool(np.array_equal(pi, pip)), " value diff:", round(float(np.abs(V - Vp).max()), 6))

    Q, returns = q_learning_tabular(mdp, episodes=3000)
    print("mean return first 200:", round(float(np.mean(returns[:200])), 2))
    print("mean return last 200 :", round(float(np.mean(returns[-200:])), 2))
    print("Q-policy agrees with VI:", round(float((Q.argmax(1) == pi).mean()), 3))

"""Module 15 — a real environment and a DQN, in Gymnasium + PyTorch."""
import random
from collections import deque

import gymnasium as gym
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn


class QNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, n_actions, hidden=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(obs_dim, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, n_actions),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)


def train_dqn(env_name="CartPole-v1", episodes=300, gamma=0.99, lr=1e-3,
              batch=64, eps_start=1.0, eps_end=0.05):
    env = gym.make(env_name)
    obs_dim = env.observation_space.shape[0]
    n_actions = env.action_space.n

    policy_net = QNet(obs_dim, n_actions)
    target_net = QNet(obs_dim, n_actions)
    target_net.load_state_dict(policy_net.state_dict())   # the target-network trick
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(policy_net.parameters(), lr=lr)
    buffer = deque(maxlen=50_000)                          # experience replay

    returns = []
    for ep in range(episodes):
        obs, _ = env.reset(seed=ep)
        eps = max(eps_end, eps_start - ep / (0.6 * episodes))
        total = 0.0
        for t in range(500):
            if random.random() < eps:
                a = env.action_space.sample()
            else:
                with torch.no_grad():
                    a = int(policy_net(torch.tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32)).argmax())
            obs2, r, terminated, truncated, _ = env.step(a)
            done = terminated or truncated
            buffer.append((obs, a, r, obs2, done))
            total += r
            obs = obs2

            if len(buffer) >= batch:
                sample = random.sample(buffer, batch)
                s, ac, rew, s2, d = zip(*sample)
                s = torch.tensor(np.array(s), dtype=torch.float32)
                ac = torch.tensor(ac)
                rew = torch.tensor(rew, dtype=torch.float32)
                s2 = torch.tensor(np.array(s2), dtype=torch.float32)
                d = torch.tensor(d, dtype=torch.float32)
                q = policy_net(s).gather(1, ac[:, None]).squeeze(1)
                with torch.no_grad():
                    q_next = target_net(s2).max(1).values
                    target = rew + gamma * q_next * (1 - d)
                loss = nn.functional.smooth_l1_loss(q, target)
                opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
                nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(policy_net.parameters(), 10.0)
                opt.step()
            if done:
                break
        returns.append(total)
        if ep % 20 == 0:
            target_net.load_state_dict(policy_net.state_dict())
            print(f"episode {ep:3d}  return {total:6.1f}  eps {eps:.2f}")
    env.close()
    return policy_net, returns


if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    random.seed(0)
    np.random.seed(0)
    net, returns = train_dqn(episodes=200)
    print("mean return, first 20 episodes:", round(float(np.mean(returns[:20])), 1))
    print("mean return, last 20 episodes :", round(float(np.mean(returns[-20:])), 1))
    print("\nCartPole is solved above ~475; compare with the random baseline of ~22.")
The NumPy block makes the comparison exact by building the full transition tensor $T$: value iteration, policy iteration and tabular Q-learning all recover the same policy, and the first two agree on values to $10^{-6}$. The PyTorch block is the modern version of the same idea, with a neural network replacing the table.

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

What does the Markov property assert about an MDP?

$\Pr(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) = \Pr(s_{t+1}\mid s_0,\dots,s_t,a_0,\dots,a_t)$: the state is a sufficient statistic for the future, which is what makes the Bellman recursion valid.

Q2

The Bellman operator is a contraction with modulus $\gamma \lt 1$. This guarantees:

By the Banach fixed-point theorem, a contraction has a unique fixed point and iterating it converges at rate $\gamma^k$ from any starting point.

Q3

Q-learning is called off-policy because:

The target is the greedy continuation, so it estimates $Q^\*$ even while the data comes from an exploring policy — hence convergence to the optimal policy from suboptimal behaviour.

Q4

Fixed $\epsilon$-greedy exploration has which property?

Coverage is guaranteed (good for convergence of estimates) but it keeps taking random actions forever, so it pays a constant per-step penalty — linear regret. Decaying $\epsilon$ is the standard remedy.

Q5

The "deadly triad" that can make RL diverge is:

Each is fine alone; together they break the contraction guarantee. DQN's replay buffer and target network are direct mitigations.

$(S, A, T, R, \gamma)$: states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, discount. Add a start state and optional terminal states.

Definition
$V^\*(s) = \max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')[R+\gamma V^\*(s')]$. One-step lookahead, and the fixed point of a contraction.
Term

Definition
$r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$: the difference between the one-step estimate and the current value. The quantity every TD and Q-learning update moves along.
Term
On-policy vs. off-policy
Definition
On-policy (SARSA) evaluates the policy being followed. Off-policy (Q-learning) evaluates the greedy policy while following another.
Term
Discount factor
Definition
$\gamma$: makes the return finite for $\gamma\lt1$ and sets the effective planning horizon $1/(1-\gamma)$.
Term
Regret
Definition
$T V^\*(s_0) - \sum_t r_t$: reward lost relative to acting optimally from the start. Minimising it requires learning efficiently, not just eventually.
Term
DQN's two stabilisers
Definition
Experience replay (decorrelates samples) and a target network (stops the target from chasing itself). Both address the deadly triad.
Term
Exploration bonus
Definition
Add a term rewarding unfamiliar states (count-based or curiosity-driven) instead of relying on random $\epsilon$. Reduces regret substantially.

Practice problems

Easy

One Bellman backup

A state has two actions. Action A leads to a state with $V=10$ with probability 1 and gives reward 0. Action B gives reward 5 and leads to a state with $V=2$ with probability 1. With $\gamma=0.9$, which action is better and what is $V(s)$?

$Q(s,A) = 0 + 0.9(10) = 9$. $Q(s,B) = 5 + 0.9(2) = 6.8$.

Action A is better, and $V(s) = 9$. Note that the immediate reward favours B but the discounted future dominates — that is exactly what $\gamma$ controls.

Medium

Trace a Q-learning update

With $\alpha=0.5$, $\gamma=0.9$, $Q(s,a)=2$, a transition giving reward 1 into state $s'$ where $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')=6$, and the episode continuing, compute the new $Q(s,a)$. What if the transition is terminal?

Continuing: target $= 1 + 0.9(6) = 6.4$; TD error $= 6.4 - 2 = 4.4$; new $Q = 2 + 0.5(4.4) = 4.2$.

Terminal: the bootstrap term is dropped, target $= 1$; TD error $= 1 - 2 = -1$; new $Q = 2 - 0.5 = 1.5$. The value decreases — the state was over-valued because it was assumed to lead somewhere good.

Hard

Prove the contraction property

Show that the Bellman operator is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm, and use it to bound $\|V_k - V^\*\|_\infty$ after $k$ sweeps.

  1. Let $U, W$ be value functions. Using $|\max_a f(a)-\max_a g(a)|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$: $$ \|\mathcal{B}U-\mathcal{B}W\|_\infty \le \gamma\max_s\max_a\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')|U(s')-W(s')|. $$
  2. Since $\sum_{s'}T(s,a,s')=1$ and $|U(s')-W(s')|\le\|U-W\|_\infty$, the right side is $\le\gamma\|U-W\|_\infty$. ∎
  3. Iterating: $\|V_k-V^\*\| = \|\mathcal{B}V_{k-1}-\mathcal{B}V^\*\| \le \gamma\|V_{k-1}-V^\*\| \le \gamma^k\|V_0-V^\*\|$.
  4. Practical consequence: to guarantee $\|V_k-V^\*\|\le\epsilon$ you need $k\ge\log(\|V_0-V^\*\|/\epsilon)/\log(1/\gamma)$ sweeps — a number that grows sharply as $\gamma\to1$, which is why high-discount problems need many more iterations.

6 Portfolio project

Project 15

Solve, then learn, then generalise

Take one environment through all three levels of the RL stack — exact planning, tabular learning, and function approximation — and measure what each buys and costs.

Brief

  1. Define an MDP of your own (a grid world with a twist, or a small operations problem). Write out $S$, $A$, $T$, $R$, $\gamma$.
  2. Solve it exactly with value iteration and policy iteration; report sweep counts, wall-clock time and the optimal policy.
  3. Solve it again with tabular Q-learning; plot learning curves over several seeds and compare the learned policy with the exact one.
  4. Now hide the model and use a linear function approximator on hand-designed features. Show the approximation error as a function of the feature set.
  5. Run an $\epsilon$-decay experiment and plot regret for fixed vs. decaying $\epsilon$.

Deliverables

  • Environment specification and exact solution
  • Learning curves with confidence bands over 5 seeds
  • Regret plot comparing exploration schedules
