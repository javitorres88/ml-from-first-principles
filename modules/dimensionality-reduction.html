Module 13 · Unsupervised

Dimensionality Reduction & Representation Learning

SVD and low-rank approximation, PCA as a change of basis, explained variance, LSA, t-SNE and UMAP for visualisation, then autoencoders — including the proof that a linear autoencoder *is* PCA.

📚 Source: Lecture Notes 11 ⏱ 80 min 🧪 3 interactive labs 🐍 3 implementations

◎ Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Compute the SVD of a small matrix and interpret left/right singular vectors.
  • Derive PCA from both the maximum-variance and minimum-reconstruction-error viewpoints.
  • Explain why centering makes singular values equal √(n−1) times the standard deviations.
  • Prove that a linear autoencoder with squared error learns the PCA subspace.
  • Distinguish PCA, kernel PCA, t-SNE and autoencoders by the structure they preserve.

1 Intuition

Representations, and why fewer dimensions can be better

A representation is an embedding — a mapping of a data modality into another mathematical space that captures the features relevant to a task. Deep networks learn one implicitly (which is why transfer learning works), but we can also learn one directly, without labels. There are three unsupervised routes:

RouteAssumptionMethods
Structure-basedthe data lies on a low-dimensional subspace or manifold of a high-dimensional ambient spacePCA, SVD, LSA, LLE, Isomap
Autoencodersa good representation is one you can reconstruct fromlinear, nonlinear, denoising, sparse, variational
Density estimationlearn $p(\mathbf{x})$ and sample from itGANs, diffusion models

Two practical goals drive dimensionality reduction:

  • Compression / modelling — fewer inputs, less noise, faster training, less overfitting.
  • Visualisation — a 2-D or 3-D embedding a human can look at.

Singular value decomposition

For an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$, the SVD is

$$ A = U\Sigma V^\top $$

with $U$ and $V$ orthogonal and $\Sigma$ diagonal with non-negative singular values $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r\ge 0$. The names come from what $A$ does to a vector:

$$ A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i $$

so $A$ maps each right singular vector to the corresponding left singular vector, stretched by $\sigma_i$. Equivalently,

$$ A = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top $$

a sum of rank-1 matrices ordered by importance. Truncating that sum after $k$ terms gives the best rank-$k$ approximation in the Frobenius norm — a theorem (Eckart–Young), not a heuristic. That single fact is why SVD underlies PCA, latent semantic analysis, recommender systems and naive image compression.

Principal component analysis

PCA is a change of basis: rotate the coordinate system so the first axis points along the direction of greatest variance, the second along the next-greatest orthogonal direction, and so on. The new coordinates are linearly uncorrelated by construction.

The procedure via SVD:

  1. Compute the mean $\mu_i$ (and, if standardising, the standard deviation $\sigma_i$) of each feature.
  2. Centre (or standardise) the data. Centering is required — otherwise the singular values describe the second moment about the origin, not the variance.
  3. Take the SVD of the centred matrix: $X = U\Sigma V^\top$.
  4. The principal components are the columns of $U\Sigma$, i.e. $\mathrm{PC}_i = \mathbf{u}_i\sigma_i$.
  5. The variance of component $i$ is $\sigma_i^2/(n-1)$; the fraction of variance explained is $\sigma_i^2/\sum_j\sigma_j^2$.
Centre or standardise?

If the features share units and ranges (the iris measurements are all centimetres), centering preserves the relative importance of the features. If they do not — income in dollars next to age in years — you must standardise, or the largest-variance feature will define PC1 regardless of its relevance. After standardising, the squared singular values divided by $n$ describe the correlation matrix instead of the covariance.

PCA's assumptions, and what to use instead

  • It captures only linear correlations. For non-linear structure, use kernel PCA or an autoencoder.
  • It assumes mean and variance are enough to describe the distribution — i.e. roughly Gaussian data. Independent component analysis relaxes this when the sources are non-Gaussian.
  • Centering and projection produce negative values, which may be meaningless for counts or intensities. Non-negative matrix factorisation is the alternative.

Visualisation: t-SNE and UMAP

PCA is a global linear projection; for visualisation you usually want to preserve local neighbourhoods instead. t-SNE computes a probability distribution over pairs of points in the high-dimensional space (closer points get higher probability), then finds a low-dimensional layout whose pairwise probabilities match it, using a heavy-tailed (t) distribution in the embedding to avoid crowding. Two caveats: it is stochastic — every run differs — and distances between clusters are not meaningful, only the clusters themselves. UMAP is a related method that is faster and preserves more global structure; it is approximately equivalent to t-SNE applied after a PCA initialisation.

Autoencoders

An autoencoder learns a compressed representation by learning to reconstruct:

$$ f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H} \text{ (encoder)}, \qquad g:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{X} \text{ (decoder)}, \qquad \arg\min_{f,g}\;\mathbb{E}\big[L\big(\mathbf{x},\,(g\circ f)(\mathbf{x})\big)\big] $$

The simplest version has one hidden layer, linear activations, and squared error: $L = \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\|^2$ with $\mathbf{x}' = UV\mathbf{x}$. If the bottleneck dimension $K \ge D$ you can trivially set $UV = I$; if $K \lt D$ the output is forced into a $K$-dimensional subspace. What is the best such subspace? The one minimising the squared distance from the data to its projections — which is the same as maximising the variance of the projections. That is PCA, and section 2.4 proves it.

Adding non-linear activations buys representations PCA cannot express. The standard variants:

VariantMechanismPurpose
Undercompletehidden size < input sizeforce compression
Overcompletehidden size > input sizeneeds regularisation, else it learns the identity
Denoisingfeed a corrupted input, reconstruct the clean onelearn robust features
Sparse$\ell_1$ penalty on the codeinterpretable, sparse activations
Convolutionalconvs + transposed convsimages; the basis of latent diffusion
Variationalencode a distribution, sample, decodegeneration; the encoder inside Stable Diffusion

A useful sanity check on what compression means: an image set of size $128\times128\times3$ with 8-bit colour has $256^{49152}$ possible members — vastly more than the number of atoms in the universe. The set of natural images is a vanishingly thin subset, and an autoencoder with a small bottleneck can only ever produce points in that subset. A good autoencoder's output space approximates the natural-image manifold; a bad one produces blurry averages.

Discriminative vs. generative models

ModelLearnsCan do
Discriminative$p(y\mid\mathbf{x})$classify; no notion of whether an input is plausible
Generative$p(\mathbf{x})$sample, detect outliers, reject unreasonable inputs, learn features without labels
Conditional generative$p(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$generate data conditioned on a label — built from the other two via Bayes' rule

GANs are implicit generative models: a generator maps a noise vector $\mathbf{z}$ to a sample $G(\mathbf{z})$, and a discriminator tries to tell real from fake. Training is the minimax game

$$ \min_G\max_D\; J_D = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\text{data}}\big[-\log D(\mathbf{x})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\big[-\log\big(1-D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\big] $$

with the generator using the non-saturating variant $J_G = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[-\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))]$ to avoid flat gradients when its samples are obviously fake. GANs produce crisp images but give no likelihood, so you cannot measure how well they model the distribution — the motivation for diffusion models in module 16.

Real-world examples

Genomics · visualisation

The first two principal components of a gene-expression matrix usually separate tissue types — the standard first figure in a genomics paper.

Build a term–document matrix, truncate its SVD, and documents with similar meaning end up with similar vectors even when they share no words.

Train an autoencoder on normal data; a new sample with high reconstruction error is anomalous. The same trick detects fraud and equipment faults.

Two generators and two discriminators learn to translate between unpaired image collections (horses ↔ zebras, maps ↔ aerial photos), with a cycle-consistency loss to prevent content loss.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Computing the SVD from eigen-decompositions

Derivation — left and right singular vectors from $AA^\top$ and $A^\top A$
  1. Both $A^\top A$ ($n\times n$) and $AA^\top$ ($m\times m$) are symmetric positive semi-definite, so each can be diagonalised by an orthonormal basis with non-negative eigenvalues.
  2. Let $A^\top A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. Then $AA^\top(A\mathbf{v}) = A(A^\top A\mathbf{v}) = \lambda(A\mathbf{v})$, so $A\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $AA^\top$ with the same eigenvalue.
  3. Normalising gives $\mathbf{u} = A\mathbf{v}/\sqrt\lambda$, hence $A\mathbf{v} = \sigma\mathbf{u}$ with $\sigma = \sqrt\lambda$. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues, and $U$ and $V$ have the same eigenvalues because the two matrices are similar. ∎
  4. Worked example. $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&2\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}$ gives $A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $8, 2$ and eigenvectors $(1,1),(-1,1)$. Normalising and applying $A\mathbf{v}=\sigma\mathbf{u}$ gives $\sigma_1 = 2\sqrt2$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt2$, $U = I$, so $$ A = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2&0\\0&\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\\-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\end{bmatrix}. $$

2.2 Low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young)

Truncating the SVD after $k$ terms gives

$$ A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=k+1}^{r}\sigma_i^2 $$

and no rank-$k$ matrix can do better in the Frobenius norm. This is why the singular-value spectrum is the right stopping criterion: the tail sum $\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2$ is the squared reconstruction error. Lab 2 plots the spectrum and lets you cut it — the "energy kept" readout is $1 - \sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2$.

2.3 PCA as variance maximisation

Derivation — the first principal component maximises projected variance

Let $X$ be the centred $n\times d$ data matrix and $\mathbf{w}$ a unit vector.

  1. The projected values are $X\mathbf{w}$; their sample variance is $$ \frac{1}{n-1}\|X\mathbf{w}\|^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{w}. $$
  2. Let $S = \frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$ be the sample covariance. Maximise $\mathbf{w}^\top S\mathbf{w}$ subject to $\|\mathbf{w}\|=1$.
  3. Form the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{w}^\top S\mathbf{w} - \lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w}-1)$ and differentiate: $$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{w}} = 2S\mathbf{w} - 2\lambda\mathbf{w} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; S\mathbf{w} = \lambda\mathbf{w}. $$
  4. So the optimum is an eigenvector of $S$, and the achieved variance is $\mathbf{w}^\top S\mathbf{w} = \lambda$. Maximising therefore picks the largest eigenvalue. ∎
  5. Subsequent components maximise the same objective subject to orthogonality to the previous ones, which yields the remaining eigenvectors in decreasing eigenvalue order.
  6. Since $S = \frac{1}{n-1}V\Sigma^2V^\top$, the eigenvectors of $S$ are the right singular vectors of $X$ and the variances are $\sigma_i^2/(n-1)$ — connecting this derivation to the SVD route.

2.4 A linear autoencoder learns the PCA subspace

Proof — the optimal linear autoencoder spans the top-$K$ principal subspace
  1. With linear encoder and decoder the reconstruction is $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = UV\mathbf{x}$ where $V\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$ and $U\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K}$. The matrix $UV$ has rank at most $K$ and is a projection-like map.
  2. The loss is $\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}-UV\mathbf{x}\|^2$. For any fixed $K$-dimensional subspace $\mathcal{S}$, the best rank-$K$ map is the orthogonal projection onto $\mathcal{S}$ — any other map would leave a larger residual for some direction.
  3. So the problem reduces to choosing the subspace: minimise the expected squared distance from the data to its projection onto $\mathcal{S}$.
  4. By Pythagoras, $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \|\mathrm{proj}_\mathcal{S}\mathbf{x}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathrm{proj}_\mathcal{S}\mathbf{x}\|^2$, and $\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$ is fixed. Minimising the residual is therefore the same as maximising $\mathbb{E}\|\mathrm{proj}_\mathcal{S}\mathbf{x}\|^2$, the variance captured by the subspace.
  5. By §2.3 that maximum is attained by the span of the top $K$ eigenvectors of the covariance — the PCA subspace. Choose $U = Q$ and $V = Q^\top$ for an orthonormal basis $Q$ of that subspace. ∎
  6. Lab 3 shows the learned decoder direction converging onto PC1: the autoencoder rediscovers PCA without ever computing a covariance matrix.

2.5 What t-SNE optimises

t-SNE defines a similarity distribution in the input space and one in the embedding, then minimises their KL divergence:

$$ p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|^2/2\sigma_i^2\big)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp\!\big(-\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_k\|^2/2\sigma_i^2\big)}, \qquad q_{ij} = \frac{(1+\|\mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\ne l}(1+\|\mathbf{y}_k-\mathbf{y}_l\|^2)^{-1}} $$
$$ \mathrm{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} $$

The bandwidth $\sigma_i$ is chosen per point so that the perplexity (effective number of neighbours) matches a hyperparameter. The heavy-tailed Student-$t$ kernel in $Q$ allows moderately distant points to be modelled cheaply, solving the "crowding problem" where a 2-D map cannot separate many clusters. Because the objective is asymmetric ($p\log(p/q)$ penalises putting $q$ mass where $p$ has none but not the reverse), t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods and is unreliable about global distances — which is why cluster sizes and inter-cluster gaps in a t-SNE plot should not be over-interpreted.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 13 — PCA via power iteration, and a linear autoencoder, no libraries."""
import math
import random


def mean_centre(X):
    d = len(X[0])
    mu = [sum(row[j] for row in X) / len(X) for j in range(d)]
    return [[row[j] - mu[j] for j in range(d)] for row in X], mu


def covariance(Xc):
    n, d = len(Xc), len(Xc[0])
    return [[sum(Xc[i][a] * Xc[i][b] for i in range(n)) / (n - 1) for b in range(d)] for a in range(d)]


def matvec(M, v):
    return [sum(m * x for m, x in zip(row, v)) for row in M]


def norm(v):
    return math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v))


def power_iteration(M, iters=200):
    """Leading eigenvector and eigenvalue of a symmetric PSD matrix."""
    d = len(M)
    v = [1.0 / math.sqrt(d)] * d
    lam = 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        w = matvec(M, v)
        n = norm(w)
        v = [x / n for x in w]
        lam = n
    return v, lam


def pca(X, k=2):
    Xc, mu = mean_centre(X)
    S = covariance(Xc)
    comps, variances = [], []
    d = len(S)
    for _ in range(k):
        v, lam = power_iteration(S)
        comps.append(v)
        variances.append(lam)
        # deflate: remove the component we just found
        S = [[S[a][b] - lam * v[a] * v[b] for b in range(d)] for a in range(d)]
    total = sum(variances)  # variance captured by the k components
    return comps, variances, mu


def project(X, comps, mu):
    return [[sum((row[j] - mu[j]) * c[j] for j in range(len(row))) for c in comps] for row in X]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(0)
    X = [[2 * rng.gauss(0, 1) + rng.gauss(0, 0.3),
          rng.gauss(0, 1) - 0.4 * rng.gauss(0, 0.3)] for _ in range(300)]

    comps, variances, mu = pca(X, k=2)
    print("component 1:", [round(v, 3) for v in comps[0]], " variance:", round(variances[0], 3))
    print("component 2:", [round(v, 3) for v in comps[1]], " variance:", round(variances[1], 3))
    print("explained ratio:", round(variances[0] / (variances[0] + variances[1]), 3))
    print("orthogonal:", round(sum(a * b for a, b in zip(comps[0], comps[1])), 10))

    Z = project(X, comps, mu)
    print("projected shape:", len(Z), "x", len(Z[0]))

"""Module 13 — PCA via SVD, low-rank image compression, and a linear autoencoder."""
import numpy as np


def pca_svd(X, k=None):
    """Centre, take the SVD, return components, explained variance and the transform."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    mu = X.mean(0)
    Xc = X - mu
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
    var = S ** 2 / (len(X) - 1)
    k = k or len(S)
    return {
        "components": Vt[:k],                 # (k, d) rows are PC directions
        "singular_values": S[:k],
        "explained_variance": var[:k],
        "explained_ratio": var[:k] / var.sum(),
        "mean": mu,
    }


def transform(model, X):
    return (np.asarray(X, float) - model["mean"]) @ model["components"].T


def inverse_transform(model, Z):
    return np.asarray(Z, float) @ model["components"] + model["mean"]


def low_rank(A, k):
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    return (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k], S


def linear_autoencoder(X, k=1, lr=0.05, steps=400):
    """Gradient descent on ||X - X W V||^2 with W: d->k and V: k->d."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    X = X - X.mean(0)
    n, d = X.shape
    W = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.1, (d, k))
    V = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(0, 0.1, (k, d))
    for _ in range(steps):
        H = X @ W
        R = H @ V - X
        gW = X.T @ (R @ V.T) / n
        gV = H.T @ R / n
        W -= lr * gW
        V -= lr * gV
    return W, V


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    X = rng.normal(size=(300, 5)) @ np.diag([3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.2]) @ np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(5, 5)))[0]

    model = pca_svd(X)
    print("explained ratio:", np.round(model["explained_ratio"], 3))
    print("cumulative     :", np.round(np.cumsum(model["explained_ratio"]), 3))

    Z = transform(model, X)
    Xrec = inverse_transform(model, Z[:, :2])
    print("2-component reconstruction MSE:", round(float(((X - Xrec) ** 2).mean()), 4))

    # Low-rank image compression
    img = np.outer(np.sin(np.linspace(0, 6, 60)), np.cos(np.linspace(0, 4, 45)))
    img += (np.random.default_rng(1).random(img.shape) - 0.5) * 0.1
    for k in (1, 5, 20):
        rec, S = low_rank(img, k)
        err = np.linalg.norm(img - rec) / np.linalg.norm(img)
        print(f"rank {k:2d}: relative error {err:.3f}  stored {k * (img.shape[0] + img.shape[1] + 1)} vs {img.size}")

    # The linear autoencoder finds the same subspace as PCA
    W, V = linear_autoencoder(X, k=1, steps=2000)
    pca_dir = model["components"][0]
    ae_dir = V[0] / np.linalg.norm(V[0])
    cos = abs(float(pca_dir @ ae_dir))
    print("|cos(angle)| between autoencoder and PC1:", round(cos, 5))

"""Module 13 — scikit-learn: PCA, kernel PCA, t-SNE, and an autoencoder in PyTorch."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, KernelPCA, TruncatedSVD
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)          # 1797 images, 64 pixels each
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

# --- explained variance -----------------------------------------------------
pca = PCA().fit(X)
cum = np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
print("components for 80% / 95% variance:",
      int(np.searchsorted(cum, 0.80)) + 1, "/", int(np.searchsorted(cum, 0.95)) + 1)

# --- compression and reconstruction error -----------------------------------
for k in (2, 10, 20, 40):
    p = PCA(n_components=k).fit(X)
    rec = p.inverse_transform(p.transform(X))
    print(f"k={k:2d}: explained={p.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.3f}  MSE={((X - rec) ** 2).mean():.4f}")

# --- what centering vs standardising does -----------------------------------
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
Xi = load_iris().data
for label, data in (("centred", Xi - Xi.mean(0)), ("standardised", StandardScaler().fit_transform(Xi))):
    p = PCA(2).fit(data)
    print(f"iris {label:13s} PC1 explains {p.explained_variance_ratio_[0]:.3f}")

# --- kernel PCA for non-linear structure ------------------------------------
kpca = KernelPCA(n_components=2, kernel="rbf", gamma=0.02).fit_transform(X)
print("kernel PCA output shape:", kpca.shape)

# --- t-SNE for visualisation ------------------------------------------------
tsne = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, random_state=0).fit_transform(X[:500])
print("t-SNE output shape:", tsne.shape, " (stochastic: rerun to see it move)")

# --- an autoencoder in PyTorch ----------------------------------------------
import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
ae = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(64, 16), nn.ReLU(),      # encoder
    nn.Linear(16, 8), nn.ReLU(),       # bottleneck
    nn.Linear(8, 16), nn.ReLU(),       # decoder
    nn.Linear(16, 64),
)
Xt = torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(ae.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(600):
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(ae(Xt), Xt)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print("autoencoder final MSE:", round(loss.item(), 4))
print("bottleneck compression:", 64 / 8, "x")

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

The singular values of a centred data matrix $X$ relate to the covariance matrix as:

$X^\top X/(n-1) = V\Sigma^2V^\top/(n-1)$, so the eigenvalues of the covariance are $\sigma_i^2/(n-1)$ and the eigenvectors are the right singular vectors.

Q2

Why must the data be centred before PCA?

Without centring, the dominant singular vector points toward the mean rather than toward the direction of greatest spread — the classic first-principle error in PCA code.

Q3

A linear autoencoder with a $K$-dimensional bottleneck and squared error learns:

Minimising reconstruction error is equivalent to maximising captured variance, whose optimum is the span of the top $K$ eigenvectors of the covariance.

Q4

Term
SVD
Definition
$A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with $U,V$ orthogonal. $A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i$, so $A = \sum_i\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top$.
Term
Eckart–Young theorem
Definition
The truncated SVD is the optimal rank-$k$ approximation in Frobenius norm, with error $\sqrt{\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2}$. Optimal, not heuristic.
Term
Explained variance ratio
Definition
$\sigma_i^2/\sum_j\sigma_j^2$: the fraction of total variance captured by component $i$. The cumulative version tells you how many components you need.
Term
Latent semantic analysis
Definition
SVD on a term–document matrix, truncated to $k$ components. Documents with similar meaning end up close even without shared words.
Term
Undercomplete vs. overcomplete
Definition
Bottleneck smaller than the input forces compression; larger allows the identity and needs regularisation (denoising, sparsity, dropout) to learn anything useful.
Term
Implicit vs. explicit density models
Definition
GANs are implicit: they sample without giving $p(\mathbf{x})$. VAEs, autoregressive models and diffusion are explicit: you can evaluate (or bound) the likelihood.
Term
Why GAN training is unstable
Definition
Two networks optimise opposing objectives, so the loss does not decrease monotonically and can oscillate or collapse. Non-saturating generator loss and careful architecture choices help.
Term
Cycle consistency
Definition
In CycleGAN, mapping $A\to B\to A$ should return the original. It is what allows unpaired image translation without content loss.

Practice problems

Easy

PCA by hand in 2-D

Three centred points: $(-1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,-1)$. Compute the covariance matrix, its eigenvalues, and the first principal component.

$X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$, so $S = X^\top X/(n-1) = \begin{bmatrix}1&-0.5\\-0.5&1\end{bmatrix}$.

Eigenvalues: $1.5$ and $0.5$. Eigenvector for $1.5$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

PC1 explains $1.5/2 = 75\%$ of the variance. The data is more spread along the anti-diagonal, which is visible by inspection.

Medium

Prove the reconstruction-error identity

Show that for centred data, truncating after $k$ components leaves squared reconstruction error $\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2$.

  1. Write $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ and $X_k = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top$ (first $k$ components).
  2. Because $U$ has orthonormal columns, $\|X - X_k\|_F^2 = \|U^\top(X-X_k)\|_F^2$.
  3. $U^\top X = \Sigma V^\top$ and $U^\top X_k$ keeps only the first $k$ rows of $\Sigma V^\top$; the difference is the remaining rows, whose squared Frobenius norm is $\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2$. ∎
  4. Since the residual only involves the discarded singular values, and no rank-$k$ matrix can have a smaller residual, this is optimal.
Hard

Show that a linear autoencoder recovers PCA

Let $\hat X = XWV$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, $V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$. Show that minimising $\|X-XWV\|_F^2$ over rank-$k$ factorisations is equivalent to PCA, and characterise the optimal $WV$.

  1. $WV$ has rank at most $k$, so the problem is: find the best rank-$k$ approximation of $X$.
  2. By Eckart–Young the optimum is $X_k = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top$.
  3. Hence the optimal composite map is $WV = V_k^\top V_k$ — the orthogonal projection onto the top-$k$ principal subspace (up to a change of basis inside the subspace, which does not change the projection). ∎
  4. So a linear autoencoder can learn the PCA subspace but not the PCA axes or the ordered variances: any invertible linear map inside the bottleneck gives the same reconstruction. That is exactly what lab 3 shows — the decoder direction converges onto PC1's span.
