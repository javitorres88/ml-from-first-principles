Module 09 · Unsupervised

Unsupervised Learning: Clustering

k-means as alternating minimisation of within-cluster variance, k-means++, the elbow method, density-based DBSCAN, and agglomerative clustering with four linkage criteria.

📚 Source: Lecture Notes 6 ⏱ 70 min 🧪 3 interactive labs 🐍 3 implementations

◎ Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • State the k-means objective and prove each of the two update steps never increases it.
  • Explain k-means++ seeding and its O(log k) approximation guarantee.
  • Apply the elbow method and silhouette-style reasoning to choose k.
  • Classify points as core, border or noise and run DBSCAN by hand.
  • Build a dendrogram for a small dataset under single, complete, average and Ward linkage.

1 Intuition

Learning without labels

In unsupervised learning the training data are not labelled into predefined classes — and we do not even know how many classes there are. Instead, the algorithm uses similarity and dissimilarity across features to generate group labels called clusters. A model trained this way either reveals useful structure (clusters) or learns a representation of the data.

FamilyMethodsWhat it produces
Clusteringk-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical, Gaussian mixturesgroup assignments
Representation learningself-organising maps, autoencoders (module 13)a lower-dimensional embedding
Anomaly detectionlocal outlier factor, angle-based outlier detectionoutlier scores

Three families of clustering

  • Centroid-based — clusters are sets of points closest to some central point (k-means).
  • Density-based — clusters are regions of higher density than their surroundings (DBSCAN).
  • Hierarchical — clusters are nested, with one criterion defining distance between points and another (the linkage) defining distance between clusters.

Clustering may also be fuzzy (no sharp boundaries) or partial (some points left unassigned). The choice of similarity metric is as consequential as the algorithm:

FamilyMetricUse when
Distance$\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|_2^2$, $\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|_1$numeric features, comparable scales
Distribution$\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)^\top S^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)}$ (Mahalanobis)correlated features, $S$ positive definite
Angle$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j}{\|\mathbf{x}_i\|\|\mathbf{x}_j\|}$text, embeddings, high dimension

k-means: alternate, and repeat

Given a dataset $X=\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N\}$ with $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^M$, k-means finds a partition $P=\{p_1,\dots,p_K\}$ minimising the within-cluster sum of squares:

$$ \sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{m}_j\|_2^2, \qquad \mathbf{m}_j = \frac{1}{|p_j|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\mathbf{x} $$

Each centre $\mathbf{m}_j$ is just the arithmetic mean of its partition. The algorithm alternates two steps:

  1. Initialise centres $\mathbf{m}_1,\dots,\mathbf{m}_K$ at random.
  2. Assign every sample to its nearest centre.
  3. Update each centre to the mean of the samples now assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until no sample changes class.

Both steps provably never increase the objective (section 2.1), and the objective is bounded below by zero, so k-means always terminates. It terminates at a local minimum, which is why initialisation matters so much.

k-means++

Instead of random centres, choose the first uniformly at random, then choose each subsequent centre with probability proportional to $d(\mathbf{x})^2$, the squared distance to the nearest existing centre. Far-away points are very likely to be chosen, which spreads the initial centres out. On average this yields clusters within $O(\log k)$ of the optimal solution. Switch the initialisation in lab 1 and watch the final inertia change.

How many clusters?

Sometimes the problem dictates $k$: will this student graduate (2 groups), or is the weather risk low/medium/high (3 groups). Otherwise, too small a $k$ fails to partition the data meaningfully and too large a $k$ overfits. The elbow method plots the within-cluster variance against $k$ and looks for the point where additional clusters stop buying much reduction. Lab 2 plots it alongside the silhouette score, which is usually more decisive.

The warning in the lecture

On the original sample, $k=4$ minimised within-cluster variance; on an expanded dataset containing the same structure, $k=4$ produced errors while $k=2$ was correct. A lower within-cluster variance is always achievable with more clusters — it is a training metric, and it will happily reward overfitting. This is exactly the lesson of module 01, applied to an unsupervised problem.

Where k-means fails

  • Only a local minimum is guaranteed, and the result depends on initialisation.
  • Outliers drag centroids, because the mean is not robust.
  • $k$ must be known in advance.
  • Assumes convex, isotropic, similarly-sized clusters — it cannot handle heteroscedasticity (different covariances), non-spherical shapes, or nested structures.

Those last failures are what DBSCAN and hierarchical clustering exist to fix, and lab 3 makes them visible.

DBSCAN

DBSCAN classifies every point by the density of its neighbourhood, using two hyperparameters: a radius Eps and a count MinPts.

Point typeDefinition
Coreat least MinPts points within Eps — interior of a dense region
Borderfewer than MinPts within Eps, but inside the neighbourhood of a core point
Noiseneither core nor border

Two core points within Eps of each other are connected by a density edge; a point is density-connected to another if a path of such edges exists. The algorithm: label points, discard noise, then for each unassigned core point start a new cluster containing everything density-connected to it, and finally attach border points to the cluster of their nearest core point.

Tuning follows the same logic: within a cluster, each point's $k$-th nearest neighbour is at roughly the same distance, while noise points have a much larger one. Sort every point's $k$-th-neighbour distance and look for a knee — set Eps at the knee and MinPts to $k$.

Hierarchical clustering

  • Agglomerative (bottom-up) — each point starts as its own cluster; repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until one remains.
  • Divisive (top-down) — start with everything; repeatedly split until $k$ clusters or singletons remain.

Both operate on a pairwise proximity matrix, and the key design decision is the linkage criterion:

LinkageDistance between clustersStrengthWeakness
Minimum (single)closest pair of pointshandles non-elliptical shapessensitive to noise; chaining
Maximum (complete)farthest pairless sensitive to noisebreaks large clusters, biased to globular shapes
Group averageaverage over all cross pairscompromisebiased to globular shapes
Centroid (Ward's)increase in SSE when mergedhierarchical analogue of k-means; can initialise k-meansbiased to globular shapes

The result is visualised as a dendrogram, a tree recording the order and height of merges. Complexity is $O(N^2)$ space for the proximity matrix and $O(N^3)$ time in the naive implementation (reducible to $O(N^2\log N)$). Two structural limitations: a merge can never be undone, and no objective function is being directly minimised.

HDBSCAN removes the need to fix Eps: it runs DBSCAN across a range of epsilons, defining each point's core distance via its $k$-th nearest neighbour, and prunes a minimum spanning tree of the reachability graph. It is scale-invariant and more robust than DBSCAN at the cost of more computation.

Real-world examples

Medicine · phenotypes

Patient subgroups

Cluster longitudinal vitals to discover sepsis phenotypes that no one had defined a priori. The clusters then become features (or labels) for supervised models.

Retail · segmentation

Customer groups

k-means on recency/frequency/monetary features produces the segments marketing teams target. Standardisation is mandatory — otherwise revenue outvotes frequency.

Geoscience · hotspots

Earthquake and crime hotspots

DBSCAN on coordinates finds arbitrarily shaped hotspots and explicitly labels isolated events as noise — exactly the behaviour a centroid method cannot provide.

Bioinformatics · gene families

Hierarchical clustering

Expression heatmaps with dendrograms are standard in genomics: the tree shows which genes group together at every scale, and the analyst chooses the cut height.

Vision · colour quantisation

k-means in RGB space

Cluster pixel colours into $k$ centroids to compress an image — the same algorithm as image compression via SVD (module 13), with a different geometry.

NLP · topic discovery

Document clustering

Cluster TF-IDF or embedding vectors with cosine similarity to discover topics. k-means with Euclidean distance on unnormalised vectors behaves very differently.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Why k-means never gets worse

Proof — each step minimises the objective given the other

Write the objective as $J(P, M) = \sum_{j}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2$.

  1. Assignment step. Fix the centres. The objective is a sum over samples, and each sample's term depends only on its own assignment, so minimising jointly means minimising each independently: $$ \mathbf{x}\in p_{j^\*} \text{ where } j^\* = \arg\min_j \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2. $$ Reassigning any point to a different cluster can only increase its term, so $J$ cannot increase. ∎
  2. Update step. Fix the partition. The objective separates across clusters, so minimise each $\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2$ independently. Differentiate with respect to $\mathbf{m}_j$: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{m}_j}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j)^\top(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j) = -2\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{m}_j = \frac{1}{|p_j|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\mathbf{x}. $$ ∎
  3. This is block coordinate descent: alternate exact minimisation over one block while holding the other fixed. Every iteration decreases a bounded-below objective, so the sequence converges — to a local minimum, since the joint problem is not convex (the assignment is discrete).

2.2 The objective is the total within-cluster variance

Expand the squared distance to the mean:

$$ \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2 = \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 - |p_j|\,\|\mathbf{m}_j\|^2 = \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 $$

so minimising the objective is exactly minimising the sum of the per-cluster variances (unnormalised). It is also equivalent to maximising the between-cluster variance, since the total variance decomposes:

$$ \underbrace{\sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\sum_j |p_j|\,\|\mathbf{m}_j - \bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2}_{\text{between}} + \underbrace{\sum_j\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2}_{\text{within}} $$

This is the ANOVA decomposition, and it explains the name "variance-based clustering" and why $k$-means is described as minimising intra-cluster variance.

2.3 k-means++ seeding

Let $D(\mathbf{x})$ be the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to the nearest centre chosen so far. The seeding procedure:

  1. Choose $\mathbf{m}_1$ uniformly at random from $X$.
  2. For $i = 2,\dots,k$: choose $\mathbf{m}_i = \mathbf{x}'$ with probability $\dfrac{D(\mathbf{x}')^2}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}D(\mathbf{x})^2}$.
  3. Run standard k-means from those centres.

Because the probability is proportional to the squared distance, points far from all existing centres are overwhelmingly likely to be picked, so the initial centres are spread out. Arthur and Vassilvitskii proved that the resulting objective is, in expectation, within $8(\ln k + 2)$ of the optimal — the $O(\log k)$ guarantee quoted in the lecture notes.

2.4 Silhouette score

For a point $\mathbf{x}$ in cluster $C_i$, let $a(\mathbf{x})$ be its mean distance to the other members of $C_i$ and $b(\mathbf{x})$ its mean distance to the members of the nearest other cluster. Then

$$ s(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{b(\mathbf{x}) - a(\mathbf{x})}{\max\{a(\mathbf{x}), b(\mathbf{x})\}} \in [-1, 1] $$

Values near 1 mean the point is much closer to its own cluster than to any other; near 0 means it sits on a boundary; negative means it is probably in the wrong cluster. Averaging over all points gives a single quality score that — unlike inertia — does not automatically improve as $k$ grows, which is why it is a better guide for choosing $k$.

2.5 DBSCAN's formal definitions

Let $N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x}) = \{\mathbf{q} : \mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{q}) \le \epsilon\}$.

  • $\mathbf{x}$ is a core point if $|N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x})| \ge \text{MinPts}$.
  • $\mathbf{q}$ is directly density-reachable from $\mathbf{x}$ if $\mathbf{q}\in N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{x}$ is core.
  • $\mathbf{q}$ is density-reachable from $\mathbf{x}$ if there is a chain $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{p}_0,\dots,\mathbf{p}_n=\mathbf{q}$ with each $\mathbf{p}_{i+1}$ directly density-reachable from $\mathbf{p}_i$.
  • $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{q}$ are density-connected if both are density-reachable from a common core point.
  • A cluster is a maximal set of density-connected points; everything else is noise.

Two consequences worth remembering: clusters can be any shape (because connectivity, not distance to a centre, defines membership), and a single density threshold cannot handle clusters of very different densities — the reason HDBSCAN exists. Complexity is $O(N\log N)$ with a spatial index and $O(N^2)$ in the worst case.

2.6 Linkage as a recurrence

Agglomerative clustering repeatedly merges the closest pair according to the linkage. Each linkage can be expressed as an update rule on distances (the Lance–Williams recurrence), which is why all four variants share one algorithm:

$$ d(A\cup B, C) = \alpha_A d(A,C) + \alpha_B d(B,C) + \beta\, d(A,B) + \gamma\,|d(A,C) - d(B,C)| $$
Linkage$\alpha_A$$\alpha_B$$\beta$$\gamma$
Single1/21/20−1/2
Complete1/21/20+1/2
Average$|A|/(|A|+|B|)$$|B|/(|A|+|B|)$00
Ward$(|A|+|C|)/(|A|+|B|+|C|)$$(|B|+|C|)/(|A|+|B|+|C|)$$-|C|/(\cdot)$0

Ward's linkage is the one that pairs naturally with k-means: both minimise within-cluster sum of squares, so a Ward dendrogram cut at $k$ clusters is an excellent initialisation for k-means.

3 Interactive visualizations

Lab 1 · k-means

Alternating assignment and update

Press Step to advance one iteration: the ✕ marks are centroids, the thin lines connect each point to its centre. Inertia falls at every step and the centroid movement shrinks toward zero — that is the block-coordinate descent of §2.1 converging.

Now switch initialisation to random and press New seed a few times. Different starts land in different local minima with visibly different inertia. k-means++ rarely does this, which is why it is the default everywhere.

Lab 2 · choosing k

Elbow, silhouette, and overfitting

The upper plot shows inertia (normalised, lower is better) and silhouette (higher is better) against $k$; the lower plot shows the clustering at the chosen $k$.

Inertia decreases monotonically forever — it is a training metric and will always reward more clusters. The silhouette peaks at the true $k=3$ and then declines. Use the elbow as a first guess and the silhouette as the decision rule; the verdict readout names the regime.

Lab 3 · density

Core, border and noise on two moons

k-means cannot separate two interleaving crescents — no set of centroids works. DBSCAN can, because it follows density connectivity rather than distance to a centre. Filled points are core, hollow points are border, grey points are noise.

Shrink eps and the clusters fragment into noise; grow it and the two moons merge into one cluster. This sensitivity is DBSCAN's real weakness — one global density threshold cannot serve clusters of different densities, which is exactly what HDBSCAN addresses.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 09 — k-means, k-means++ and DBSCAN, no libraries."""
import math
import random


def dist2(a, b):
    return sum((ai - bi) ** 2 for ai, bi in zip(a, b))


def kmeans(X, k, iters=100, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    centers = [list(p) for p in rng.sample(X, k)]          # naive init
    labels = [-1] * len(X)
    for _ in range(iters):
        new_labels = [min(range(k), key=lambda j: dist2(x, centers[j])) for x in X]
        if new_labels == labels:
            break
        labels = new_labels
        for j in range(k):
            members = [x for x, l in zip(X, labels) if l == j]
            if members:
                centers[j] = [sum(c) / len(members) for c in zip(*members)]
    inertia = sum(dist2(x, centers[l]) for x, l in zip(X, labels))
    return centers, labels, inertia


def kmeans_pp(X, k, iters=100, seed=0):
    """k-means++ seeding, then standard Lloyd iterations."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    centers = [list(X[rng.randrange(len(X))])]
    while len(centers) < k:
        d2 = [min(dist2(x, c) for c in centers) for x in X]
        total = sum(d2)
        r, acc, pick = rng.random() * total, 0.0, 0
        for i, v in enumerate(d2):
            acc += v
            if acc >= r:
                pick = i
                break
        centers.append(list(X[pick]))
    return kmeans_from(X, centers, iters)


def kmeans_from(X, centers, iters=100):
    k = len(centers)
    labels = [-1] * len(X)
    for _ in range(iters):
        labels = [min(range(k), key=lambda j: dist2(x, centers[j])) for x in X]
        new = []
        for j in range(k):
            members = [x for x, l in zip(X, labels) if l == j]
            new.append([sum(c) / len(members) for c in zip(*members)] if members else centers[j])
        if all(dist2(a, b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(new, centers)):
            break
        centers = new
    inertia = sum(dist2(x, centers[l]) for x, l in zip(X, labels))
    return centers, labels, inertia


def dbscan(X, eps, min_pts):
    n = len(X)
    neighbours = [[j for j in range(n) if j != i and math.dist(X[i], X[j]) <= eps] for i in range(n)]
    core = [len(nb) + 1 >= min_pts for nb in neighbours]
    labels = [-1] * n
    cluster = 0
    for i in range(n):
        if not core[i] or labels[i] != -1:
            continue
        stack = [i]
        labels[i] = cluster
        while stack:
            cur = stack.pop()
            if not core[cur]:
                continue
            for j in neighbours[cur]:
                if labels[j] == -1:
                    labels[j] = cluster
                    stack.append(j)
        cluster += 1
    return labels, cluster


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(0)
    X = [[rng.gauss(0, 0.4), rng.gauss(0, 0.4)] for _ in range(40)]
    X += [[3 + rng.gauss(0, 0.4), rng.gauss(0, 0.4)] for _ in range(40)]

    for name, fn in (("random init", kmeans), ("k-means++", kmeans_pp)):
        _, _, inertia = fn(X, 2, seed=3)
        print(f"{name:12s} inertia = {inertia:.3f}")

    labels, k = dbscan(X, eps=1.0, min_pts=4)
    print("dbscan clusters:", k, " noise:", labels.count(-1))

"""Module 09 — vectorised k-means with k-means++, plus the elbow/silhouette sweep."""
import numpy as np


def kmeans(X, k, iters=300, seed=0, init="++"):
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = len(X)

    if init == "++":
        centers = [X[rng.integers(n)]]
        for _ in range(k - 1):
            d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - np.array(centers)[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).min(1)
            probs = d2 / d2.sum()
            centers.append(X[rng.choice(n, p=probs)])
        C = np.array(centers)
    else:
        C = X[rng.choice(n, k, replace=False)]

    labels = np.full(n, -1)
    for _ in range(iters):
        D = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)      # (n, k)
        new = D.argmin(1)
        if np.array_equal(new, labels):
            break
        labels = new
        for j in range(k):
            if np.any(labels == j):
                C[j] = X[labels == j].mean(0)
    inertia = float(((X - C[labels]) ** 2).sum())
    return C, labels, inertia


def silhouette(X, labels):
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    n = len(X)
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None, :] - X[None]) ** 2).sum(-1))
    scores = []
    for i in range(n):
        same = labels == labels[i]
        same[i] = False
        if not same.any():
            continue
        a = D[i, same].mean()
        b = min(D[i, labels == c].mean() for c in np.unique(labels) if c != labels[i])
        scores.append((b - a) / max(a, b))
    return float(np.mean(scores))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    X = np.vstack([rng.normal(c, 0.5, (60, 2)) for c in ([0, 0], [3, 0], [1.5, 2.5])])

    print(" k   inertia   silhouette")
    for k in range(2, 8):
        C, labels, inertia = kmeans(X, k, seed=1)
        print(f"{k:2d}  {inertia:8.2f}  {silhouette(X, labels):10.3f}")

    # Repeated restarts: local minima are real
    inertias = [kmeans(X, 3, seed=s, init="random")[2] for s in range(10)]
    print("\nrandom-init inertia over 10 seeds: min %.2f max %.2f" % (min(inertias), max(inertias)))
    inertias = [kmeans(X, 3, seed=s, init="++")[2] for s in range(10)]
    print("k-means++ inertia over 10 seeds : min %.2f max %.2f" % (min(inertias), max(inertias)))

"""Module 09 — scikit-learn clustering: k-means, DBSCAN, agglomerative."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering, DBSCAN, KMeans
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs, make_moons
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score, silhouette_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

# ---- k-means on blobs ------------------------------------------------------
X, y = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=4, cluster_std=0.9, random_state=0)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

for k in range(2, 8):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(f"k={k}: inertia={km.inertia_:8.2f}  silhouette={silhouette_score(X, km.labels_):.3f}")

km = KMeans(n_clusters=4, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
print("\nARI vs. truth:", round(adjusted_rand_score(y, km.labels_), 3))
print("cluster sizes:", np.bincount(km.labels_))

# ---- the classic failure: two moons ---------------------------------------
Xm, ym = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.06, random_state=0)
print("\nk-means on moons   silhouette:", round(silhouette_score(Xm, KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xm)), 3))
print("DBSCAN on moons    silhouette:", round(silhouette_score(Xm, DBSCAN(eps=0.15, min_samples=5).fit_predict(Xm)), 3))

# ---- DBSCAN hyperparameter sweep ------------------------------------------
for eps in (0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.25, 0.4):
    lab = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=5).fit_predict(Xm)
    print(f"eps={eps:.2f}: clusters={len(set(lab)) - (1 if -1 in lab else 0)}  noise={(lab == -1).sum()}")

# ---- hierarchical clustering with different linkages ----------------------
for linkage in ("single", "complete", "average", "ward"):
    lab = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage=linkage).fit_predict(Xm)
    print(f"{linkage:9s} ARI vs. truth = {adjusted_rand_score(ym, lab):.3f}")

# ---- choosing eps from the k-distance curve -------------------------------
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
kdist = np.sort(NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=5).fit(Xm).kneighbors(Xm)[0][:, -1])
print("\nk-distance at 90th percentile (candidate eps):", round(float(np.percentile(kdist, 90)), 3))
The NumPy block prints the experiment that motivates k-means++: across ten random seeds, random initialisation produces a range of final inertias, while k-means++ almost always lands on the same (better) one.

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

The k-means update $\mathbf{m}_j = \frac{1}{|p_j|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\mathbf{x}$ is optimal because:

$\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{m}}\sum(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m})^2 = -2\sum(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}) = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf{m} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}$. The guarantee is only a local minimum of the joint problem.

Q2

Why can k-means not separate two interleaving crescent shapes?

Centroid-based assignment always produces convex cells. A crescent is not convex, so no set of centroids can recover it — a density-based or hierarchical method is required.

Q3

k-means++ chooses each new centre with probability proportional to:

Probability $\propto d(\mathbf{x})^2$ spreads the centres out, giving an expected objective within $O(\log k)$ of optimal.

Q4

In DBSCAN, a point with fewer than MinPts neighbours within Eps, but lying inside the Eps-neighbourhood of a core point, is:

Border points sit on the edge of a dense region: not dense enough to be core, but reachable from a core point. They are attached after the clusters have grown from the core points.

Q5

Which linkage is the hierarchical analogue of k-means?

Ward's criterion is the increase in within-cluster sum of squares when two clusters merge — the same quantity k-means minimises. Cutting a Ward dendrogram is therefore a good way to initialise k-means.

$\sum_j\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2$: the within-cluster sum of squares (inertia / scatter). Minimising it is equivalent to maximising between-cluster variance.
Term
Block coordinate descent
Definition
Alternate exact minimisation over two blocks of variables. k-means alternates assignment and centroid update, each exactly optimal given the other.
Term
Elbow method
Definition
Plot inertia against $k$ and choose the point where the decrease flattens. A heuristic: inertia always decreases, so it can only suggest, never decide.
Term
Silhouette score
Definition
$s = (b-a)/\max(a,b)$ with $a$ the mean intra-cluster distance and $b$ the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Ranges $[-1,1]$; unlike inertia it penalises too many clusters.
Term
Core / border / noise
Definition
DBSCAN's three point types: at least MinPts neighbours within Eps; reachable from a core point; neither.
Term
Dendrogram
Definition
A tree recording the sequence and height of merges (or splits) in hierarchical clustering. Cutting it at a chosen height gives a flat clustering.
Term
Chaining
Definition
Single linkage's failure mode: a chain of closely spaced points bridges two genuine clusters. The reason single linkage is noise-sensitive.
Term
HDBSCAN
Definition
Runs DBSCAN over a range of Eps values by building a minimum spanning tree of the mutual-reachability graph and pruning high-weight edges. Scale-invariant and robust to varying density.

Practice problems

Easy

One k-means iteration by hand

Points: $(0,0), (0,2), (4,0), (4,2)$. Initial centres $\mathbf{m}_1=(0,0)$, $\mathbf{m}_2=(4,2)$. Run one assignment step and one update step; compute the inertia before and after.

Assignment. $(0,0)\to1$ (d=0), $(0,2)\to1$ (d=2), $(4,0)\to2$ (d=2), $(4,2)\to2$ (d=0).

Update. $\mathbf{m}_1 = (0,1)$, $\mathbf{m}_2 = (4,1)$.

Inertia before: $0+4+4+0 = 8$. After: $1+1+1+1 = 4$. The objective halved in one iteration, and the next assignment changes nothing — the algorithm has converged.

Medium

Prove the variance decomposition

Show that $\sum_i\|\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 = \sum_j|p_j|\|\mathbf{m}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 + \sum_j\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\|^2$.

  1. Write $\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{m}_{j(i)}) + (\mathbf{m}_{j(i)} - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$, where $j(i)$ is the cluster of point $i$.
  2. Expand the square and sum: $\sum_i\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{m}_{j(i)}\|^2 + \sum_i\|\mathbf{m}_{j(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 + 2\sum_i(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{m}_{j(i)})^\top(\mathbf{m}_{j(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})$.
  3. The cross term vanishes, because grouping by cluster gives $\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in p_j}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j) = |p_j|\mathbf{m}_j - |p_j|\mathbf{m}_j = 0$. ∎
  4. Regrouping the second term by cluster gives $\sum_j|p_j|\|\mathbf{m}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2$.
Hard

Prove that k-means clusters are convex

Show that each k-means cluster is contained in the Voronoi cell of its centroid, and conclude that a non-convex cluster can never be recovered exactly.

  1. The assignment step sets $p_j = \{\mathbf{x} : \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_j\| \le \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_i\|\;\forall i\}$.
  2. Squaring and cancelling $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$ gives the linear inequality $2\mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{m}_i-\mathbf{m}_j) \le \|\mathbf{m}_i\|^2-\|\mathbf{m}_j\|^2$.
  3. Each inequality defines a closed half-space, so $p_j$ is an intersection of half-spaces — a convex polytope. ∎
  4. Hence a crescent, a ring, or two interleaved spirals can never be a single k-means cluster, regardless of initialisation or $k$.

Project 09

Discovering structure nobody labelled

Run an honest clustering study on real, unlabelled data and defend every choice you make — including the choice of $k$.

Brief

  1. Choose an unlabelled dataset with a plausible group structure (customer data, patient vitals, or a public dataset whose labels you reveal only at the end).
  2. Implement k-means with random and k-means++ initialisation, plus DBSCAN, from scratch.
  3. Standardise features and justify the metric choice; show what changes if you skip standardisation.
  4. Sweep $k = 2\ldots10$ and report inertia, silhouette and gap-statistic curves. Choose $k$ with an explicit rule.
  5. Compare k-means, DBSCAN and Ward hierarchical clustering; use ARI against withheld labels as a final check, not as a tuning signal.
  6. Describe each cluster in domain terms: what distinguishes its members, and how large is it?

Deliverables

  • Notebook with the selection curves and the comparison table
  • From-scratch implementations with tests against scikit-learn
  • Cluster profile table and a one-page interpretation for a domain reader
