1 Intuition

Decision trees

A decision tree predates machine learning — it comes from operations research — and it is the only model in this course you can print out and hand to a domain expert. It partitions the feature space by dichotomizing one feature at a time: "is petal width ≤ 0.8?" is a yes/no question that splits the data into two groups. The learning task is to decide which questions to ask and in what order.

The dominant algorithm is CART (Classification and Regression Tree), which is what scikit-learn implements. It grows the tree greedily:

  1. While the stopping criteria are not met:
  2. For every impure leaf in the tree:
  3. Choose the best feature $k$ and threshold $t_k$ according to the splitting criterion.
  4. Split the data into two subspaces using $(k, t_k)$.

Stopping criteria are maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf, or a minimum impurity decrease. Because splits are axis-aligned, the resulting decision boundary is a staircase — visible in lab 1 as a mosaic of rectangles.

What makes a good split?

CART chooses $(k, t_k)$ to minimise the size-weighted impurity of the two children:

$$ J(k, t_k) = \frac{m_{\text{left}}}{m}\,G_{\text{left}} + \frac{m_{\text{right}}}{m}\,G_{\text{right}} $$

where $G$ measures the impurity of a node and $m$ is the node's sample count. Three criteria are common:

CriterionFormulaRange (2 classes)Notes
Gini$1 - \sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i^2$$[0, 0.5]$default; cheap, works well
Entropy$-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i$$[0, 1]$information gain; slightly more expensive
Misclassification$1 - \max_i p_i$$[0, 0.5]$less sensitive to small changes; rarely used for growing

Gini has a precise meaning: it is the probability that a randomly chosen sample would be incorrectly labelled if labels were drawn at random from the node's class distribution. Section 2.1 derives this.

Once built, a tree estimates probabilities by sending a sample down to a leaf and returning the class frequencies observed there during training. On iris, a fully grown tree's leaves are pure, so it returns 100% or 0% — a sign that it is overfitting and should be pruned or depth-limited.

Ensembles: many weak models

Ensemble learning trains multiple models and combines their outputs. Because it is applied on top of other methods, it is also called meta-learning. The goal is to turn weak learners into a strong one; the simplest combiner is majority voting (a hard method), while averaging probabilities or using learned weights are soft methods.

Why averaging helps — the probabilistic argument

A biased coin lands heads 51% of the time. Flip it 1,000 times and the average approaches 0.51 by the law of large numbers. Treat each flip as a model and the average as the ensemble: the aggregate is more reliable than any single member. The catch is the phrase "independent": if all the models make the same mistakes, averaging does nothing. That is precisely why bagging resamples and random forests subsample features — both are ways of decorrelating the members.

The theoretical backing for boosting is computational learning theory. In the PAC framework (Valiant, 1984) a concept class is strongly learnable if a learner can, with high probability, output a hypothesis that is correct on all but an arbitrarily small fraction of instances. Schapire (1990) proved that any strongly learnable concept can be learned by weak learners — those that do slightly better than chance. Boosting is the constructive version of that theorem.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Gini impurity is an expected error rate

Derivation — why $1-\sum p_i^2$ measures impurity
  1. Take a node with class proportions $p_1,\dots,p_K$. Pick a sample at random and assign it a label drawn at random from the node's distribution.
  2. The probability of a correct guess is $$ \Pr(\text{correct}) = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\Pr(\text{pick class } i)\Pr(\text{guess } i) = \sum_i p_i\cdot p_i = \sum_i p_i^2. $$
  3. So the error probability is $1 - \sum_i p_i^2$, which is exactly the Gini impurity. ∎
  4. Sanity checks. A pure node ($p_1 = 1$) gives $1-1 = 0$. A balanced two-class node gives $1 - 2(1/4) = 0.5$, its maximum.
  5. Entropy has the same shape but a different justification: it is the expected number of bits needed to encode the label, $-\sum p_i\log_2 p_i$. Both are concave in $\mathbf{p}$, which is what makes greedy splitting sensible — a split that makes children purer always decreases the weighted average.

2.2 The cost of finding a split

For a node with $m$ samples and $d$ features, CART sorts each feature ($O(m\log m)$) and evaluates up to $m-1$ thresholds. Evaluating one threshold naively costs $O(m)$, giving $O(dm^2\log m)$ per node — expensive. In practice the class counts are updated incrementally as the threshold sweeps, making one feature's scan $O(m\log m)$ and the whole node $O(dm\log m)$. Total cost for a balanced tree of depth $\log m$ is $O(dm\log^2 m)$.

2.3 Why bagging reduces variance but not bias

Derivation — the variance of an average of correlated models
  1. Let $M$ models each have variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. The average $\bar f = \frac1M\sum_m f_m$ has $$ \mathrm{Var}(\bar f) = \frac{1}{M^2}\left[\sum_m \mathrm{Var}(f_m) + \sum_{m\ne m'}\mathrm{Cov}(f_m,f_{m'})\right] = \frac{\sigma^2}{M} + \frac{M-1}{M}\rho\sigma^2. $$
  2. As $M\to\infty$ the first term vanishes and the variance tends to $\rho\sigma^2$. ∎
  3. The correlation, not the number of models, is the binding constraint. Bagging decorrelates members by training on different resamples (so $\rho$ drops), but trees trained on the same data with the same greedy splits are still highly correlated — which is exactly why random forests also subsample features at each split, pushing $\rho$ down further.
  4. Bias. Averaging cannot move the mean, so bagging leaves bias untouched. Boosting is the complement: it attacks bias by fitting successive models to the errors of the current ensemble.

2.4 AdaBoost, derived

AdaBoost maintains a weight $w^{(i)}$ for every training sample, starting uniform at $1/m$.

Derivation — the weighted error, the predictor weight, and the reweighting
  1. Weighted error of predictor $j$: $$ r_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} w^{(i)}\,\mathbb{1}\big[\hat y_j^{(i)} \ne y^{(i)}\big]}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} w^{(i)}}. $$
  2. Predictor weight: $\alpha_j = \eta\log\frac{1-r_j}{r_j}$, where $\eta$ is a learning-rate hyperparameter. A predictor with $r_j \lt 0.5$ gets $\alpha_j \gt 0$; a random predictor ($r_j = 0.5$) gets $\alpha_j = 0$; a worse-than-random one gets a negative weight. Accurate models count more.
  3. Sample reweighting: multiply the weight of each misclassified sample by $e^{\alpha_j}$, leave correct ones unchanged, then renormalise. Since $\alpha_j \gt 0$, hard samples become relatively heavier and the next predictor is forced to focus on them.
  4. Why that exact form? AdaBoost is stagewise gradient descent on the exponential loss $L = \sum_i \exp\big(-y^{(i)}F(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\big)$ where $F = \sum_j \alpha_j \hat y_j$. Adding the $j$-th predictor changes the loss to $\sum_i w^{(i)}\exp(-\alpha_j y^{(i)}\hat y_j^{(i)})$. Splitting the sum into correct and incorrect samples gives $$ = e^{-\alpha_j}\sum_{\text{correct}} w^{(i)} + e^{\alpha_j}\sum_{\text{incorrect}} w^{(i)} = e^{-\alpha_j}(1-r_j) + e^{\alpha_j}r_j \;\;(\text{after normalising } \textstyle\sum_i w^{(i)}=1). $$ Differentiating with respect to $\alpha_j$ and setting to zero: $$ -e^{-\alpha_j}(1-r_j) + e^{\alpha_j}r_j = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{2\alpha_j} = \frac{1-r_j}{r_j} \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha_j = \tfrac12\log\frac{1-r_j}{r_j}. $$ ∎
  5. Prediction: for classification, $\hat y(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_k \sum_{j} \{\alpha_j : \hat y_j(\mathbf{x}) = k\}$; for regression, the weighted sum $\sum_j \alpha_j\hat y_j(\mathbf{x})$.

The default base estimator in scikit-learn's AdaBoostClassifier is a decision stump — a tree with a single split. If the ensemble overfits, reduce the number of estimators or regularise them; if it underfits, add estimators, reduce regularisation, or raise the learning rate slightly.

2.5 Gradient boosting

Gradient boosting is the same stagewise idea with a different target. Instead of reweighting samples, each new predictor is fitted to the residuals of the current ensemble:

$$ h_j^{(i)} = y^{(i)} - \hat y_j^{(i)}, \qquad F_{j+1} = F_j + \nu\,h_j $$

with a shrinkage factor $\nu$ (the learning rate) that damps each step. For squared error, fitting residuals is gradient descent on the loss — hence the name. For other losses you fit the negative gradient, which is why gradient boosting generalises to logistic loss, Poisson loss and ranking losses.

2.6 Random forests and feature importance

A random forest is bagging over trees plus a random subset of features considered at each split (typically $\sqrt{d}$ for classification, $d/3$ for regression). The extra randomness decorrelates the trees, which — by the variance formula above — is what actually buys the improvement.

Random forests also produce an importance score: for each feature, average the impurity decrease it produced across all nodes where it was used, weighted by the number of samples reaching those nodes. This supports wrapper-based feature selection, but be careful: impurity importance is biased toward high-cardinality features, and permutation importance is usually a better choice.

Gini, entropy and split quality

All three criteria are maximised at $p = 0.5$ (maximum confusion) and zero at the extremes (pure nodes). Gini and half-entropy are almost indistinguishable in shape, which is why the choice rarely matters in practice; misclassification is piecewise linear and therefore flat in places, giving no gradient signal — that is why it is a poor criterion for growing a tree.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 08 — CART (Gini) and AdaBoost stumps, from scratch."""
from collections import Counter


def gini(labels):
    n = len(labels)
    if n == 0:
        return 0.0
    counts = Counter(labels)
    return 1.0 - sum((c / n) ** 2 for c in counts.values())


def weighted_impurity(left, right):
    n = len(left) + len(right)
    return (len(left) * gini(left) + len(right) * gini(right)) / n


def best_split(X, y):
    """Exhaustive search over features and midpoints. Returns (feature, threshold, score)."""
    best = None
    for f in range(len(X[0])):
        values = sorted(set(row[f] for row in X))
        for a, b in zip(values, values[1:]):
            thr = (a + b) / 2
            left = [yi for row, yi in zip(X, y) if row[f] <= thr]
            right = [yi for row, yi in zip(X, y) if row[f] > thr]
            if not left or not right:
                continue
            score = weighted_impurity(left, right)
            if best is None or score < best[2]:
                best = (f, thr, score)
    return best


class Stump:
    """A depth-1 tree: one feature, one threshold, two labels."""

    def fit(self, X, y, sample_weight=None):
        w = sample_weight or [1.0] * len(y)
        best = None
        for f in range(len(X[0])):
            values = sorted(set(row[f] for row in X))
            for a, b in zip(values, values[1:]):
                thr = (a + b) / 2
                err = sum(wi for row, yi, wi in zip(X, y, w) if (row[f] <= thr) != (yi == 1))
                err = min(err, sum(w) - err)          # allow either polarity
                if best is None or err < best[0]:
                    best = (err, f, thr)
        _, self.f, self.thr = best
        left = [yi for row, yi in zip(X, y) if row[self.f] <= self.thr]
        right = [yi for row, yi in zip(X, y) if row[self.f] > self.thr]
        self.left = Counter(left).most_common(1)[0][0] if left else 1
        self.right = Counter(right).most_common(1)[0][0] if right else 0
        return self

    def predict(self, X):
        return [self.left if row[self.f] <= self.thr else self.right for row in X]


class AdaBoost:
    def __init__(self, n_estimators=20, lr=1.0):
        self.n_estimators, self.lr, self.models = n_estimators, lr, []

    def fit(self, X, y):
        n = len(y)
        w = [1.0 / n] * n
        for _ in range(self.n_estimators):
            m = Stump().fit(X, y, w)
            pred = m.predict(X)
            r = sum(wi for wi, pi, yi in zip(w, pred, y) if pi != yi) / sum(w)
            r = min(max(r, 1e-10), 1 - 1e-10)
            alpha = self.lr * 0.5 * __import__("math").log((1 - r) / r)
            w = [wi * (2.718281828 ** (alpha if pi != yi else -alpha)) for wi, pi, yi in zip(w, pred, y)]
            total = sum(w)
            w = [wi / total for wi in w]
            self.models.append((m, alpha))
            if r < 1e-10:
                break
        return self

    def predict(self, X):
        scores = [0.0] * len(X)
        for m, alpha in self.models:
            for i, p in enumerate(m.predict(X)):
                scores[i] += alpha * (1 if p == 1 else -1)
        return [1 if s >= 0 else 0 for s in scores]

    X = [[0.1], [0.2], [0.3], [0.7], [0.8], [0.9], [0.45], [0.55]]
    y = [0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1]

    print("adaboost preds:", boost.predict(X))
    print("accuracy:", sum(p == t for p, t in zip(boost.predict(X), y)) / len(y))

"""Module 08 — vectorised impurity, a recursive CART, and bagging."""
import numpy as np


def gini_from_counts(counts):
    """counts: (..., K) array of class counts -> Gini along the last axis."""
    counts = np.asarray(counts, float)
    n = counts.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
    p = np.divide(counts, n, out=np.zeros_like(counts), where=n > 0)
    return 1.0 - (p ** 2).sum(-1)


def best_split(X, y, n_classes):
    """Vectorised scan: for each feature, sort once and sweep thresholds."""
    n, d = X.shape
    best = (None, None, np.inf)
    for f in range(d):
        order = np.argsort(X[:, f])
        xs, ys = X[order, f], y[order]
        onehot = np.zeros((n, n_classes))
        onehot[np.arange(n), ys] = 1
        cum = np.cumsum(onehot, axis=0)              # counts in the left child
        total = cum[-1]
        for i in range(n - 1):
            if xs[i] == xs[i + 1]:
                continue
            left, right = cum[i], total - cum[i]
            score = (left.sum() * gini_from_counts(left) + right.sum() * gini_from_counts(right)) / n
            if score < best[2]:
                best = (f, (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2, score)
    return best


def grow(X, y, depth=0, max_depth=3, min_samples=5, n_classes=2):
    counts = np.bincount(y, minlength=n_classes)
    leaf = {"leaf": True, "label": int(counts.argmax()), "n": len(y)}
    if depth >= max_depth or len(y) < 2 * min_samples or gini_from_counts(counts) == 0:
        return leaf
    f, thr, _ = best_split(X, y, n_classes)
    if f is None:
        return leaf
    mask = X[:, f] <= thr
    if mask.sum() < min_samples or (~mask).sum() < min_samples:
        return leaf
    return {
        "leaf": False, "f": f, "thr": thr, "n": len(y),
        "left": grow(X[mask], y[mask], depth + 1, max_depth, min_samples, n_classes),
        "right": grow(X[~mask], y[~mask], depth + 1, max_depth, min_samples, n_classes),
    }


def predict_tree(node, X):
    out = np.empty(len(X), int)
    for i, row in enumerate(X):
        cur = node
        while not cur["leaf"]:
            cur = cur["left"] if row[cur["f"]] <= cur["thr"] else cur["right"]
        out[i] = cur["label"]
    return out


def bagged_forest(X, y, n_trees=25, max_depth=4, n_features=None, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    n_features = n_features or max(1, int(np.sqrt(d)))
    trees = []
    for _ in range(n_trees):
        idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)                       # bootstrap sample
        feats = rng.choice(d, n_features, replace=False)  # random feature subspace
        tree = grow(X[idx][:, feats], y[idx], max_depth=max_depth, n_classes=2)
        trees.append((tree, feats))
    return trees


def forest_predict(trees, X):
    votes = np.zeros((len(X), 2))
    for tree, feats in trees:
        for i, p in enumerate(predict_tree(tree, X[:, feats])):
            votes[i, p] += 1
    return votes.argmax(1)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    X = rng.normal(size=(300, 4))
    y = ((X[:, 0] + 0.5 * X[:, 1] ** 2 + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=300)) > 0.5).astype(int)
    tree = grow(X, y, max_depth=3, n_classes=2)
    print("single tree accuracy:", (predict_tree(tree, X) == y).mean().round(3))
    forest = bagged_forest(X, y, n_trees=40)
    print("forest accuracy     :", (forest_predict(forest, X) == y).mean().round(3))

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

A node contains 40 samples: 30 of class A and 10 of class B. Its Gini impurity is:

$p_A = 0.75$, $p_B = 0.25$, so Gini $= 1 - (0.75^2 + 0.25^2) = 1 - 0.625 = 0.375$.

Q2

Bagging primarily reduces which term of the expected error?

Averaging cannot move the mean, so bias is untouched; it lowers variance toward $\rho\sigma^2$, where $\rho$ is the correlation between members. Boosting is the bias-reducing counterpart.

Q3

In AdaBoost, a base classifier with weighted error $r = 0.5$ receives weight $\alpha = \eta\log\frac{1-r}{r}$ equal to:

$\log(0.5/0.5) = \log 1 = 0$. Errors below 0.5 give positive weight; errors above 0.5 give negative weight (the classifier is worse than chance and gets inverted).

Q4

Why does a random forest subsample features at each split, beyond bagging rows?

Variance of the average is $\rho\sigma^2 + \sigma^2(1-\rho)/M$, so the floor is set by the correlation $\rho$. Feature subsampling forces trees to differ, lowering $\rho$.

Q5

Gradient boosting fits each new tree to:

Each stage performs a gradient-descent step in function space: fit $h_j$ to $-\partial L/\partial F$, then add $\nu h_j$ to the ensemble. For MSE the negative gradient is exactly the residual $y - \hat y$.

Practice problems

Easy

Choose the better split

A node has 20 samples (12 positive, 8 negative). Split A produces children (9 pos, 1 neg) and (3 pos, 7 neg). Split B produces (8 pos, 2 neg) and (4 pos, 6 neg). Which does CART prefer under Gini?

Parent Gini $= 1 - (0.6^2+0.4^2) = 0.48$.

Split A: left $n=10$, $p=0.9 \Rightarrow G=1-(0.81+0.01)=0.18$; right $n=10$, $p=0.3 \Rightarrow G=1-(0.09+0.49)=0.42$. Weighted: $\frac{10}{20}(0.18)+\frac{10}{20}(0.42) = 0.30$.

Split B: left $G=1-(0.64+0.04)=0.32$; right $G=1-(0.16+0.36)=0.48$. Weighted: $0.40$.

CART prefers Split A (0.30 < 0.40). Note that A is preferred even though it creates one very mixed child: the objective is a weighted average, so a pure child can pay for a messy one.

Medium

Derive AdaBoost's α

Using the exponential loss $L(\alpha) = e^{-\alpha}(1-r) + e^{\alpha}r$, minimise over $\alpha$ and show that the second derivative is positive.

  1. $\frac{dL}{d\alpha} = -e^{-\alpha}(1-r) + e^{\alpha}r = 0 \Rightarrow e^{2\alpha} = \frac{1-r}{r} \Rightarrow \alpha^\* = \frac12\log\frac{1-r}{r}$.
  2. $\frac{d^2L}{d\alpha^2} = e^{-\alpha}(1-r) + e^{\alpha}r \gt 0$ for all $\alpha$ (both terms positive since $0\lt r\lt1$), so the stationary point is a minimum. ∎
  3. The loss is a convex combination of two exponentials in $\alpha$, hence strictly convex — which is why AdaBoost's per-round subproblem has a closed-form solution.
Hard

Variance of a bagged ensemble

Derive $\mathrm{Var}(\bar f) = \frac{\sigma^2}{M} + \frac{M-1}{M}\rho\sigma^2$ and compute the limit as $M\to\infty$ for $\rho = 0.2$, $\sigma^2 = 1$. What does this say about the marginal value of the 100th tree versus the 2nd?

  1. $\mathrm{Var}(\frac1M\sum_m f_m) = \frac{1}{M^2}\big[\sum_m\sigma^2 + \sum_{m\ne m'}\rho\sigma^2\big] = \frac{1}{M^2}\big[M\sigma^2 + M(M-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]$.
  2. Simplifying gives $\frac{\sigma^2}{M} + \frac{M-1}{M}\rho\sigma^2 \to \rho\sigma^2 = 0.2$ as $M\to\infty$. ∎
  3. With $M=2$ the variance is $0.5 + 0.5(0.2) = 0.6$; with $M=100$ it is $0.01 + 0.99(0.2) = 0.208$. The second tree removes 40% of the variance; the hundredth removes 0.2%. Beyond a few dozen trees you are paying compute for nothing unless you can lower $\rho$ itself.
