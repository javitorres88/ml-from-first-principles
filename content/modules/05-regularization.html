1 Intuition

The problem: fitting noise

Module 01 showed the symptom — training error falls while validation error rises. This module is about the cure and about the mathematics that explains why the cure works. There are only two levers, and every practical technique is a combination of them:

  1. Constrain the model so it cannot express the noise: fewer features, lower degree, shallower trees, smaller weights.
  2. Estimate generalization honestly so you can tell whether the constraint helped: validation sets and cross-validation.

Regularization as a budget

Rather than minimising the fit error alone, minimise fit error plus a price for complexity:

$$ g(\boldsymbol\theta, f) = \text{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) + \lambda\cdot R(\boldsymbol\theta) $$

$\lambda$ is a hyperparameter that sets the exchange rate between accuracy and simplicity. Two penalties dominate:

Name$R(\boldsymbol\theta)$Geometric constraintEffect on weights
Ridge (Tikhonov)$\sum_i\theta_i^2 = \|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2$a ball (circle in 2-D)shrinks all weights smoothly; none become exactly zero
Lasso$\sum_i|\theta_i| = \|\boldsymbol\theta\|_1$a diamond (cross-polytope)sets some weights exactly to zero → sparse model, feature selection
Elastic net$\beta\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2 + \|\boldsymbol\theta\|_1$a rounded diamondsparse and stable with correlated features
Why the diamond gives exact zeros

Picture the elliptical contours of the unpenalised loss closing in on the least-squares solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$, and a constraint region that grows as $\lambda$ shrinks. The regularised solution is the first point where the two touch. A circle is smooth, so the touching point is generically on its surface with both coordinates non-zero. A diamond has corners on the axes, and corners are where expanding ellipses tend to land — so one or more coordinates become exactly zero. That is the whole of lasso's feature-selection behaviour, and lab 1 lets you watch it happen.

Bias and variance

Suppose the true label is $y = y^\* + \epsilon$ where $y^\*$ is the best achievable prediction and $\epsilon$ is noise with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$. If we could draw many training sets and fit a model $y_k$ to each, the expected error of a single fit decomposes into three parts:

$$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y - y_k)^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(y^\* - \mathbb{E}[y_k]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(y_k - \mathbb{E}[y_k])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} $$
  • Bias — how far the average model is from the truth. Rigid models are biased: a line cannot follow a sine wave.
  • Variance — how much the fitted model moves when the training data changes. Flexible models have high variance: change one point and the degree-9 polynomial changes everywhere.
  • Noise — irreducible. No model can remove it, and it sets the floor for any metric.

Increasing complexity lowers bias and raises variance. The sum is a U-shaped curve, and the minimum of that curve — not the minimum of the training error — is what you are looking for. Regularization moves you left along the curve deliberately; cross-validation tells you where the bottom is.

Cross-validation protocols, and what each protects against

ProtocolHow folds are formedUse when

The tuning protocol itself: partition into $k$ folds; for every hyperparameter combination $j$, train $k$ times and record the mean and standard deviation of the metric; choose the best $j^\*$; retrain on all the data with $j^\*$; evaluate once on the test set. Search strategies range from grid search through randomized search and Bayesian optimization to early-stopping schemes such as successive halving and Hyperband.

The one-standard-error rule

If several hyperparameters are within one standard error of the best, prefer the simplest (most regularised) of them. The differences are inside the noise of your estimate anyway, and simpler models generalize at least as well in practice. Lab 3 draws the band for you.

Real-world examples

Genomics · $p \gg n$

Lasso for biomarker selection

With 20,000 genes and 200 patients, plain least squares is undefined (infinitely many solutions). Lasso returns a handful of non-zero coefficients — a testable gene panel.

Finance · collinear factors

Ridge for correlated predictors

Market factors are highly correlated, which makes coefficients unstable. Ridge stabilises them by shrinking together; the predictions stay accurate even when individual coefficients are not identifiable.

Clinical ML · subject leakage

Group cross-validation

ARDS data has thousands of hourly records per patient. Splitting by row leaks patient identity into the test fold; stratified group $k$-fold keeps every patient in exactly one fold.

Deep learning · implicit regularization

Weight decay and dropout

Module 07 adds $\lambda\|W\|^2$ to network losses (weight decay) and randomly zeroes activations (dropout). Both are regularization; neither is guaranteed to prevent memorisation (Zhang et al., 2017).

Competitions · stacking the odds

Blending and model selection

On leaderboards, the difference between the top 20 entries is usually inside the CV standard deviation. Reporting the error bars is the difference between a result and a coin flip.

Time series · ordered splits

Never shuffle the future

For sequential data, folds must respect time: train on the past, test on the future. Shuffled $k$-fold leaks the future into the past and produces scores you cannot reproduce in production.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 Ridge regression, solved

Add an $\ell_2$ penalty to the least-squares cost and differentiate as in module 03:

$$ g(\boldsymbol\theta) = \|A\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{t}\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2 $$
Derivation — the closed form and its shrinkage behaviour
  1. Expand: $g = \boldsymbol\theta^\top A^\top A\boldsymbol\theta - 2\boldsymbol\theta^\top A^\top\mathbf{t} + \mathbf{t}^\top\mathbf{t} + \lambda\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\theta$.
  2. Differentiate: $\nabla g = 2A^\top A\boldsymbol\theta - 2A^\top\mathbf{t} + 2\lambda\boldsymbol\theta$.
  3. Set to zero: $$ \boxed{\;(A^\top A + \lambda I)\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_\lambda = A^\top\mathbf{t}\;} \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{t} $$ Adding $\lambda I$ makes the matrix strictly positive definite whenever $\lambda \gt 0$ — so the inverse always exists, even when $A^\top A$ is singular. Ridge is what makes $p \gt n$ problems solvable.
  4. Diagonalise $A^\top A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ and rotate into that basis. Componentwise, $$ \hat\theta_\lambda^{(i)} = \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + \lambda}\,\hat\theta_0^{(i)}. $$ Every component is multiplied by a factor in $[0,1)$: the estimate is shrunk toward zero, most strongly in directions with small $\lambda_i$ (the ill-conditioned directions). ∎
  5. This is why ridge fixes the exploding polynomial coefficients of module 03: the near-null directions of the Vandermonde matrix are precisely the ones that get shrunk hardest.

2.2 Lasso and soft-thresholding

With an $\ell_1$ penalty the normal equations no longer apply (the penalty is not differentiable at zero). The solution is characterised by the subgradient condition, and for an orthonormal design it reduces to soft-thresholding:

$$ \hat\theta_j^{\text{lasso}} = \operatorname{sign}(\hat\theta_j^{\text{OLS}})\cdot\max\left(|\hat\theta_j^{\text{OLS}}| - \frac{\lambda}{2},\, 0\right) $$

Read it: subtract a fixed amount from every coefficient and clamp at zero. Small coefficients vanish exactly; large ones are shifted toward zero by the same amount. Compare with ridge, which scales every coefficient by $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\lambda)$ and never reaches zero. In practice lasso is solved by coordinate descent — cycle through features, apply the soft-threshold update to each, repeat — which is exactly what lab 1 implements.

2.3 The bias–variance decomposition, proved

Derivation — expected squared error into three terms

Fix an input $\mathbf{x}$. Let $y = y^\*(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$, $\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon)=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat{y} = y_k(\mathbf{x})$ be the prediction of a model fit to training set $k$. Write $\bar{y} = \mathbb{E}_k[\hat{y}]$ for the average prediction over training sets.

  1. Add and subtract the average prediction: $$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{y})^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(y - \bar{y} + \bar{y} - \hat{y})^2\big]. $$
  2. Expand the square: $$ = \mathbb{E}\big[(y-\bar{y})^2\big] + 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\bar{y})(\bar{y}-\hat{y})\big] + \mathbb{E}\big[(\bar{y}-\hat{y})^2\big]. $$
  3. The cross term vanishes: $y$ and $\hat y$ are independent given $\mathbf{x}$ (the model never sees this $y$), so $$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y-\bar{y})(\bar{y}-\hat{y})\big] = \mathbb{E}[y-\bar{y}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\bar{y}-\hat{y}] = (y^\*-\bar{y})\cdot 0 = 0. $$
  4. The last term is the variance by definition: $\mathbb{E}[(\hat{y}-\bar{y})^2] = \mathrm{Var}(\hat y)$.
  5. The first term decomposes further, using $\mathbb{E}[y] = y^\*$: $$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y-\bar{y})^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(y-y^\*)^2\big] + (y^\* - \bar{y})^2 = \sigma^2 + \text{bias}^2. $$
  6. ∎ Collecting: $$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat y)^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}} + \underbrace{(y^\*-\bar y)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat y)}_{\text{variance}}. $$
What this buys you

Regularization is not a heuristic: it is a way of trading a small increase in bias² for a large decrease in variance, and the decomposition says exactly when that trade is favourable — when the variance term is the dominant one, i.e. when data is scarce or the model is flexible.

2.4 What cross-validation estimates, and how noisy it is

The $k$-fold estimator averages $k$ held-out errors, each computed on $N/k$ samples:

$$ \widehat{\text{Err}}_{\text{CV}} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{R}_{F_j}\big(f^{(-j)}\big) $$

Because the folds are disjoint, each $\hat{R}_{F_j}$ is an unbiased estimate of the risk of a model trained on $(k-1)N/k$ samples. Averaging $k$ such estimates reduces variance roughly by a factor of $k$ — but not exactly, because the $k$ models share $(k-2)N/k$ of their training data and are therefore positively correlated. The consequence is practical and important: the standard deviation across folds understates the true uncertainty of the CV estimate, which is why comparing models whose scores differ by less than one standard error is meaningless.

Statistical concept

Cross-validation estimates the performance of a procedure (train on $n$ samples), not of a single fitted model. The final model you ship is trained on all $N$ samples, so it should be slightly better than the CV estimate — the estimate is conservative by design.

2.5 Leakage, formally

A pipeline is a composition of steps $\mathcal{P} = f_{\text{model}} \circ f_{\text{select}} \circ f_{\text{scale}}$. Leakage occurs whenever any step is fitted using data that also appears in a held-out fold. If $F_j$ is a fold and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{fit}}$ is the set used to fit any step, correctness requires

$$ \mathcal{D}_{\text{fit}} \cap F_j = \varnothing \quad \text{for every fold } j. $$

Feature selection, imputation, scaling, target encoding and oversampling all consume labels or statistics. Wrap them in a Pipeline so that scikit-learn re-fits them inside each fold automatically. Group structure adds a second requirement: if sample $i$ and sample $i'$ share a group $g$, they must not be split across folds — $\text{group}(i) = \text{group}(i') \Rightarrow \text{fold}(i) = \text{fold}(i')$.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 05 — ridge, lasso (coordinate descent), k-fold CV — no libraries."""
import random


def transpose(M):
    return [list(r) for r in zip(*M)]


def matmul(A, B):
    Bt = transpose(B)
    return [[sum(a * b for a, b in zip(row, col)) for col in Bt] for row in A]


def solve(A, b):
    n = len(A)
    M = [row[:] + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(A)]
    for c in range(n):
        p = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][c]))
        M[c], M[p] = M[p], M[c]
        pv = M[c][c]
        if abs(pv) < 1e-12:
            continue
        M[c] = [v / pv for v in M[c]]
        for r in range(n):
            if r != c and M[r][c]:
                f = M[r][c]
                M[r] = [a - f * b_ for a, b_ in zip(M[r], M[c])]
    return [row[-1] for row in M]


def ridge(X, t, lam):
    """(X'X + lam*I) w = X't  -- no bias column, so the intercept is not penalised."""
    Xt = transpose(X)
    G = matmul(Xt, X)
    for i in range(len(G)):
        G[i][i] += lam
    return solve(G, [sum(X[i][j] * t[i] for i in range(len(X))) for j in range(len(X[0]))])


def lasso(X, t, lam, iters=200):
    """Coordinate descent with soft-thresholding. Produces exact zeros."""
    n, m = len(X), len(X[0])
    w = [0.0] * m
    col_sq = [sum(X[i][j] ** 2 for i in range(n)) / n for j in range(m)]
    for _ in range(iters):
        for j in range(m):
            rho = 0.0
            for i in range(n):
                pred = sum(X[i][k] * w[k] for k in range(m))
                rho += X[i][j] * (t[i] - pred + X[i][j] * w[j])
            rho /= n
            thr = lam / 2
            w[j] = (rho - thr) / col_sq[j] if rho > thr else ((rho + thr) / col_sq[j] if rho < -thr else 0.0)
    return w


def k_fold_indices(n, k, seed=0):
    idx = list(range(n))
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(idx)
    return [idx[i::k] for i in range(k)]


def cv_mse(X, t, lam, penalty="ridge", k=5, seed=0):
    scores = []
    for test in k_fold_indices(len(X), k, seed):
        train = [i for i in range(len(X)) if i not in set(test)]
        Xtr = [X[i] for i in train]
        ttr = [t[i] for i in train]
        w = ridge(Xtr, ttr, lam) if penalty == "ridge" else lasso(Xtr, ttr, lam)
        err = sum((t[i] - sum(X[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(len(w)))) ** 2 for i in test) / len(test)
        scores.append(err)
    mean = sum(scores) / len(scores)
    sd = (sum((s - mean) ** 2 for s in scores) / len(scores)) ** 0.5
    return mean, sd


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(0)
    X = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(4)] for _ in range(120)]
    t = [1.5 * X[i][0] - 0.8 * X[i][1] + rng.gauss(0, 0.3) for i in range(120)]

    print("ridge  l=0.1 :", [round(v, 3) for v in ridge(X, t, 0.1)])
    print("lasso  l=0.5 :", [round(v, 3) for v in lasso(X, t, 0.5)])   # some exact zeros
    for lam in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0):
        print(f"CV MSE ridge lam={lam}: {cv_mse(X, t, lam):.4f}")

"""Module 05 — vectorised ridge/lasso, the bias-variance decomposition, CV."""
import numpy as np


def ridge(X, t, lam, fit_intercept=True):
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    t = np.asarray(t, float)
    if fit_intercept:
        mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0) + 1e-12
        Xs = (X - mu) / sd
        t_mean = t.mean()
    else:
        Xs, t_mean = X, 0.0
    w = np.linalg.solve(Xs.T @ Xs + lam * np.eye(Xs.shape[1]), Xs.T @ (t - t_mean))
    return w, t_mean


def soft_threshold(v, k):
    return np.sign(v) * np.maximum(np.abs(v) - k, 0.0)


def lasso(X, t, lam, iters=500, tol=1e-6):
    """Coordinate descent; returns exact zeros for irrelevant features."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    t = np.asarray(t, float)
    n, m = X.shape
    w = np.zeros(m)
    col_sq = (X**2).sum(0) / n
    for _ in range(iters):
        w_old = w.copy()
        for j in range(m):
            r = t - X @ w + X[:, j] * w[j]
            rho = X[:, j] @ r / n
            w[j] = soft_threshold(rho, lam / 2) / col_sq[j]
        if np.max(np.abs(w - w_old)) < tol:
            break
    return w


def bias_variance_demo(degree=3, n=20, reps=200, noise=0.3, seed=0):
    """Monte-Carlo estimate of bias^2, variance and noise for a polynomial fit."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    truth = lambda x: np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)
    xs = np.linspace(0.02, 0.98, 40)
    preds = np.empty((reps, xs.size))
    for r in range(reps):
        X = rng.random(n)
        y = truth(X) + rng.normal(0, noise, n)
        coeffs = np.polyfit(X, y, degree)
        preds[r] = np.polyval(coeffs, xs)
    mean = preds.mean(0)
    bias2 = ((mean - truth(xs)) ** 2).mean()
    var = preds.var(0).mean()
    return bias2, var, noise**2


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("degree  bias^2  variance  noise  total")
    for d in range(1, 10):
        b, v, s = bias_variance_demo(degree=d)
        print(f"{d:6d}  {b:6.3f}  {v:8.3f}  {s:5.2f}  {b+v+s:6.3f}")

    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
    X = rng.normal(size=(200, 6))
    t = 2 * X[:, 0] - X[:, 2] + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 200)
    print("\nridge:", np.round(ridge(X, t, 0.5)[0], 3))
    print("lasso:", np.round(lasso(X, t, 0.3), 3), "<- exact zeros")

"""Module 05 — the production pattern: pipelines, CV, group splits, 1-SE rule."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, Ridge
from sklearn.model_selection import (GridSearchCV, GroupKFold, StratifiedGroupKFold,
                                     StratifiedKFold, cross_validate, train_test_split)
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=0)

# ---- 1. Regularisation strength, chosen by CV -------------------------------
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))
grid = {"logisticregression__C": np.logspace(-3, 2, 12)}          # C = 1/lambda
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
search = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc", return_train_score=True)
search.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("best C:", round(search.best_params_["logisticregression__C"], 4),
      " CV AUC:", round(search.best_score_, 4))

# ---- 2. The 1-SE rule ------------------------------------------------------
res = search.cv_results_
scores = res["mean_test_score"]
sds = res["std_test_score"]
best = scores.argmax()
within = np.flatnonzero(scores >= scores[best] - sds[best])
simple = within[np.argmax(res["param_logisticregression__C"][within].astype(float))]
print(f"1-SE choice: C={res['param_logisticregression__C'][simple]}  "
      f"AUC={scores[simple]:.4f} (vs best {scores[best]:.4f})")

# ---- 3. Feature selection INSIDE the fold (no leakage) ----------------------
safe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SelectKBest(f_classif, k=10),
                     LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))
print("CV with selection inside folds:",
      round(cross_validate(safe, X_tr, y_tr, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")["test_score"].mean(), 4))

# The leaky alternative is one line away, and it inflates the score:
#   selector = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=10).fit(X_tr, y_tr)   # sees ALL training labels
#   leaky = make_pipeline(selector, StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())
# Never fit a selector outside the CV loop.

# ---- 4. Grouped data needs grouped folds -----------------------------------
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(len(X_tr) // 10), 10)[: len(X_tr)]
for splitter in (StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
                 StratifiedGroupKFold(5)):
    s = cross_validate(safe, X_tr, y_tr, cv=splitter, groups=groups if "Group" in type(splitter).__name__ else None,
                       scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"{type(splitter).__name__:22s} AUC = {s['test_score'].mean():.4f}")

# ---- 5. Polynomial ridge: regularisation vs. complexity ---------------------
Xd = np.linspace(-2, 2, 100)[:, None]
td = 0.8 * Xd.ravel() ** 3 - 0.5 * Xd.ravel() + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.2, 100)
for deg in (3, 9):
    for alpha in (0.0, 1e-2, 1.0):
        m = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(deg, include_bias=False), StandardScaler(), Ridge(alpha=alpha))
        sc = cross_validate(m, Xd, td, cv=5, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
        print(f"degree={deg} alpha={alpha:<5} CV MSE = {-sc['test_score'].mean():.4f}")
Note the pattern in block 3: the only difference between the safe and leaky pipelines is where SelectKBest is fitted. That one line is the difference between a CV score you can publish and one you cannot.

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

Why does the $\ell_1$ penalty produce exact zeros while $\ell_2$ does not?

The regularised solution is where a loss contour first touches the constraint region. A diamond's corners lie on the coordinate axes, so the touching point frequently has a zero coordinate. A circle is smooth, so solutions generically have all coordinates non-zero.

Q2

In the bias–variance decomposition, increasing model complexity typically:

A more flexible model can represent the truth more closely (bias falls) but reacts more strongly to the particular training sample (variance rises). The noise term is a property of the data and never changes.

Q3

You standardise features, then run 5-fold CV. Where must the scaler be fitted?

Unsupervised does not mean leak-free: the mean and variance of the held-out fold are still information about it. The bias is small for scaling, but the same habit causes catastrophic leakage when applied to target encoding or feature selection.

Q4

Ridge solves $(A^\top A + \lambda I)\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = A^\top\mathbf{t}$. The main benefit over least squares is:

$A^\top A$ is only positive semi-definite; adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda \gt 0$ makes every eigenvalue at least $\lambda$, guaranteeing invertibility and bounding the condition number. Training error generally goes up.

Q5

Model A scores $0.842 \pm 0.031$ in CV and model B scores $0.848 \pm 0.029$. What is the defensible conclusion?

The gap (0.006) is a fifth of a standard deviation. Selecting on that difference is selecting on noise, and doing it on the test set destroys the test set's purpose.

Ridge by hand

For a single feature with $A = (1, 2, 3)^\top$ and $\mathbf{t} = (2, 4, 7)^\top$, compute the OLS and ridge ($\lambda = 2$) slopes, without an intercept.

$A^\top A = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$ and $A^\top\mathbf{t} = 2 + 8 + 21 = 31$.

OLS: $\hat w = 31/14 = 2.214$. Ridge: $\hat w_\lambda = 31/(14+2) = 1.9375$.

The shrinkage factor is $14/16 = 0.875$, and indeed $2.214 \times 0.875 = 1.937$. Shrinkage is stronger when $A^\top A$ is small — that is, when the feature carries little information.

Prove the decomposition

Let $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon)=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat y$ be an estimator independent of $\epsilon$. Prove $\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat y)^2] = \sigma^2 + (f - \mathbb{E}[\hat y])^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat y)$.

  1. Write $y - \hat y = (f - \mathbb{E}\hat y) + \epsilon + (\mathbb{E}\hat y - \hat y)$ and square.
  2. The cross terms vanish in expectation: $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\hat y - \hat y] = 0$, and by independence $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(\mathbb{E}\hat y - \hat y)] = \mathbb{E}\epsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}\hat y - \hat y) = 0$.
  3. What remains: $(f-\mathbb{E}\hat y)^2$ (deterministic), $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2]=\sigma^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}\hat y-\hat y)^2]=\mathrm{Var}(\hat y)$. ∎
Hard

Lasso as a constrained problem

Show that minimising $\|A\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf{t}\|^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_1$ is equivalent to minimising the loss subject to $\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_1 \le t$ for a suitable $t$, and explain why this produces sparsity.

  1. Let $\boldsymbol\theta^\*$ minimise the penalised objective and set $t = \|\boldsymbol\theta^\*\|_1$. Any feasible $\boldsymbol\theta$ has $\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_1 \le \lambda t$, so the penalised problem and the constrained problem share the same minimiser; the converse direction follows from Lagrangian duality / KKT with $\lambda$ as the multiplier of the active constraint.
  2. As $\lambda\to\infty$ the corresponding $t\to0$, the region shrinks to the origin, and all coefficients are zero.
  3. Sparsity: the optimum is a point of contact between a loss contour and the diamond. Faces of the diamond give generic non-zero coordinates, but its corners lie on the axes, so for a whole interval of $\lambda$ the contact point is a corner and a coordinate is exactly zero. Coefficients therefore enter and leave the model one at a time along the path. ∎
