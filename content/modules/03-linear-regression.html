1 Intuition

Linear models are the atom of machine learning

One of the fundamental building blocks of machine learning is the linear model, which solves a supervised task using a linear function of the input vector. The same object covers all three task types:

  • Regression — predict a scalar target, e.g. a stock price or a hospital length of stay.
  • Binary classification — predict a binary label, e.g. spam vs. non-spam (module 04 wraps a linear model in a sigmoid).
  • Multi-class classification — predict a discrete label among $k$ classes (module 04 uses softmax instead).

Every linear model has the same shape:

$$ y = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b $$

where $\mathbf{w}$ is the weight vector, $b$ is the bias, and together they are the model parameters. Each choice of $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ produces a different function — a different hypothesis about how the world maps inputs to outputs.

Two ways to look at the same model

Feature space: the data lives in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and the model is a line, plane or hyperplane cutting through it. Learning means rotating and shifting that surface until it passes as close as possible to the points.
Parameter space: each candidate $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ is a single point, and the cost function $J(\mathbf{w},b)$ is a landscape over that space. Learning means walking downhill on that landscape. The second view is what generalizes to neural networks — a network with a million weights is a point in a million-dimensional parameter space, and training is still just walking downhill.

Loss, residuals, and why we square

To compare hypotheses we need a number. The loss function scores one prediction:

$$ L(y, t) = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - t)^2 $$

The quantity $y - t$ is the residual — the difference between our estimate and the ground truth. The $\tfrac12$ is there purely for convenience: it cancels the factor of 2 when we differentiate. The cost function averages the loss over all $N$ training samples:

$$ J(\mathbf{w}, b) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(y^{(j)} - t^{(j)}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(j)} + b - t^{(j)}\right)^2 $$

Squaring buys three things: it is differentiable everywhere (unlike $|\cdot|$ at zero), it treats over- and under-estimates symmetrically, and — as section 2.4 proves — it is exactly what maximum likelihood gives you if the noise is Gaussian.

Why a line does not simply "solve" the data

With two points, a line is determined: $y_1 = wx_1 + b$ and $y_2 = wx_2 + b$ is a system of two equations in two unknowns with (generically) exactly one solution. With $N = 100$ noisy points, the same exercise becomes

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_N\end{bmatrix}}_{A}\begin{bmatrix} b \\ w\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_N\end{bmatrix} $$

This is an overdetermined system: more equations than unknowns, so generically it has no exact solution. Least squares asks a different, answerable question: find the $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ that makes $A\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ as close to $\mathbf{y}$ as possible,

$$ \hat{\mathbf{w}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \|A\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2 . $$

Geometrically: $A\mathbf{w}$ can only produce vectors in the column space of $A$ (a plane, when there are two parameters). The data vector $\mathbf{y}$ generally sits off that plane. The closest point in the plane is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto it, and the residual is the perpendicular dropped from $\mathbf{y}$ to the plane. That single picture is the whole of section 2.2.

Non-linear data, linear model

What if the trend is curved? Rather than abandoning linear models, we transform the features. Fitting a quadratic $y = w_2x^2 + w_1x + w_0$ is a linear problem in the coefficients — and polynomials over a field form a vector space, so after the transformation the model is linear with respect to the parameters. All the least-squares machinery applies unchanged. This trick — the feature map $\phi$ — is the conceptual ancestor of kernels (module 14) and of every hidden layer in a neural network (module 06).

The cost of the trick

Higher degree means more parameters and a design matrix whose columns are nearly collinear ($x^7$ and $x^8$ look almost the same on $[-1,1]$). The system becomes ill-conditioned: the fitted coefficients become huge and unstable, and extrapolation explodes. Lab 3 lets you watch this happen and then repairs it with a ridge penalty.

Real-world examples

Economics · trend estimation

Fitting a trend line

Least squares dates to Legendre and Gauss (1805/1809) and was invented to fit astronomical orbits and geodesic surveys. Every "line of best fit" in a spreadsheet is the same normal equation derived below.

Housing · multiple regression

Hedonic pricing

$y = w_1\cdot\text{area} + w_2\cdot\text{bedrooms} + w_3\cdot\text{distance to transit} + b$. The weights are interpretable as marginal prices, which is why linear models survive in regulated industries.

Medicine · the ARDS pipeline

Feature construction

In the ARDS case study, statistical features (mean, max, variance of vitals) and geographic features (proportion of lung opacity) are constructed and then fed to models — including linear ones — that must be auditable by clinicians.

Finance · forecasting

Predicting a scalar

Predict tomorrow's return from today's factors. Linear models are the standard baseline precisely because any non-linear model must beat them to justify its complexity.

Science · calibration

Instrument calibration

A sensor's reading is mapped to a true value with a polynomial fit. Here extrapolation stability matters more than training error, which is exactly why regularization (module 05) is standard practice.

Deep learning · the last layer

A linear head on a deep network

Almost every neural network ends in a linear layer. Understanding least squares means understanding what that final layer is doing, and why its gradient has the shape it does in module 07.

2 Mathematical foundations

2.1 The whole dataset at once

Stack the training samples as rows of a design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}$ and the targets as a vector $\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^N$. Absorb the bias by adding a column of ones (so $\mathbf{w} = \langle b, w_1,\dots,w_m\rangle$), and predictions for the entire dataset are a single matrix–vector product:

$$ \mathbf{y} = X\mathbf{w} + b\mathbf{1} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(1)} + b \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(N)} + b\end{bmatrix}, \qquad J(\mathbf{w},b) = \frac{1}{2N}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{t}\|_2^2 $$

This is why vectorized code is not an optimisation but the natural notation: one matmul evaluates the model on all $N$ samples.

2.2 The normal equations, derived twice

The minimum of a smooth function occurs at a critical point, where the gradient vanishes. Let us find it.

Derivation A — calculus
  1. Work with the bias-augmented design matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(m+1)}$ and parameter vector $\boldsymbol\theta$, so $J(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{1}{2N}(A\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{t})^\top(A\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{t})$.
  2. Expand the quadratic form: $$ J = \frac{1}{2N}\left(\boldsymbol\theta^\top A^\top A\boldsymbol\theta - 2\,\boldsymbol\theta^\top A^\top\mathbf{t} + \mathbf{t}^\top\mathbf{t}\right). $$ Cross terms merge because $\boldsymbol\theta^\top A^\top\mathbf{t}$ is a scalar and equals its own transpose.
  3. Differentiate using the two matrix-calculus rules $\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}\boldsymbol\theta^\top M\boldsymbol\theta = 2M\boldsymbol\theta$ (for symmetric $M$) and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{c}$: $$ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} J = \frac{1}{N}\left(A^\top A\boldsymbol\theta - A^\top\mathbf{t}\right). $$
  4. Set the gradient to zero and multiply through by $N$: $$ \boxed{\;A^\top A\,\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = A^\top\mathbf{t}\;} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{t}. $$ These are the normal equations. The matrix $A^\top A$ is $(m{+}1)\times(m{+}1)$ — small even when $N$ is huge, which is why this closed form is practical for modest $m$.
  5. ∎ Is it a minimum? The Hessian is $\frac{1}{N}A^\top A$, which is positive semi-definite for every $A$ because $\mathbf{v}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v} = \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0$. A convex function has no local minima other than global ones, so the critical point is the global minimum. It is unique iff $A$ has full column rank.
Derivation B — geometry (the residual is orthogonal to the column space)
  1. The set of achievable predictions is the column space $\mathrm{col}(A) = \{A\boldsymbol\theta : \boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\}$. We want the point $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = A\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ in that subspace closest to $\mathbf{t}$.
  2. Claim: the closest point is characterised by the residual $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{t} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ being orthogonal to the subspace. Suppose not; then some direction $\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{col}(A)$ has $\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{u} \ne 0$, and moving $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ a small amount $\epsilon\mathbf{u}$ changes the squared distance by $$ \|\mathbf{r} - \epsilon\mathbf{u}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{r}\|^2 - 2\epsilon\,\mathbf{r}^\top\mathbf{u} + \epsilon^2\|\mathbf{u}\|^2, $$ which decreases for small $\epsilon$ with the right sign — contradicting minimality.
  3. Orthogonality to every column of $A$ means $A^\top\mathbf{r} = 0$, i.e. $$ A^\top(\mathbf{t} - A\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; A^\top A\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = A^\top\mathbf{t}. $$ Same equation, no derivatives. Calculus and geometry agree, as they must.
  4. ∎ The fitted values are the orthogonal projection $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{t} = P\mathbf{t}$, where $P$ is the projection matrix onto $\mathrm{col}(A)$. $P$ is symmetric and idempotent ($P^2 = P$) — the algebraic fingerprint of a projection.

2.3 The pseudoinverse

When $A^\top A$ is singular (collinear features, or more features than samples) the inverse does not exist. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse $A^+$ still gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution:

$$ A\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; A^+A\mathbf{w} = A^+\mathbf{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{w} = A^+\mathbf{y}, \qquad A^+ = V\Sigma^{+}U^\top $$

where $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ is the SVD and $\Sigma^+$ inverts each non-zero singular value (and leaves zeros alone). In practice you never form the inverse: np.linalg.lstsq uses a QR or SVD factorisation, which is numerically far more stable than solving the normal equations directly. Squaring $A$ into $A^\top A$ squares the condition number — the single most common numerical mistake in hand-rolled regression code.

2.4 Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise

Why squared error and not absolute error? Because of a probabilistic assumption about how the data was generated.

Derivation — from Gaussian noise to squared error
  1. Assume the targets are the model plus independent Gaussian noise: $$ t^{(j)} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(j)} + b + \epsilon^{(j)}, \qquad \epsilon^{(j)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \text{ i.i.d.} $$
  2. Then the likelihood of one sample is $$ p(t^{(j)}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(j)};\mathbf{w},b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(t^{(j)} - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(j)} - b)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). $$
  3. Independence makes the joint likelihood a product; take the log: $$ \log L(\mathbf{w},b) = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(t^{(j)} - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(j)} - b\right)^2. $$
  4. The first term is constant and $\sigma^2 \gt 0$, so maximising the log-likelihood is the same as $$ \arg\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(t^{(j)} - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(j)} - b\right)^2 = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w},b} 2N\,J(\mathbf{w},b). $$
  5. ∎ Consequences. (i) Squared error is the right loss when noise is Gaussian; with heavy-tailed noise, absolute error (the Laplace likelihood) is better, which is why MAE is preferred for outlier-heavy targets. (ii) The fitted $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is the maximum-likelihood estimator, so it inherits asymptotic efficiency. (iii) The estimate of $\sigma^2$ is $\frac{1}{N}\sum \hat{r}_j^2$.

2.5 Solving it iteratively: gradient descent

For large $m$, forming and inverting $A^\top A$ costs $O(m^3)$ and may not fit in memory. Gradient descent instead iterates:

$$ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \alpha\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_t), \qquad \nabla J = \frac{1}{N}A^\top(A\boldsymbol\theta_t - \mathbf{t}) $$
Derivation — which learning rates converge?
  1. The cost is a quadratic with Hessian $H = \frac{1}{N}A^\top A$, symmetric positive semi-definite. Diagonalise it: $H = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_{m+1} \ge 0$.
  2. Change coordinates to the eigenbasis, $\mathbf{u} = Q^\top(\boldsymbol\theta - \hat{\boldsymbol\theta})$. Gradient descent decouples into independent scalar recursions $$ u_i^{(t+1)} = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)\,u_i^{(t)}. $$
  3. Each recursion converges iff $|1 - \alpha\lambda_i| \lt 1$, i.e. $0 \lt \alpha \lt 2/\lambda_i$, for every $i$. The binding constraint is the largest eigenvalue: $$ \boxed{\;0 \lt \alpha \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}\;} $$
  4. The convergence rate is governed by the smallest eigenvalue, so the number of iterations scales with the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$: an elongated bowl takes many steps along the shallow direction. ∎
  5. This is exactly what module 07's momentum and adaptive methods fix — and why feature scaling (which shrinks $\kappa$) speeds up gradient descent so dramatically.

2.6 Polynomial features and the Vandermonde matrix

For a degree-$d$ polynomial the design matrix is a Vandermonde matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots & x_1^d \\ \vdots & & & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_N & x_N^2 & \cdots & x_N^d \end{bmatrix} $$

With several variables, add every product and power up to degree $d$; for $d=2$ and inputs $x_1,x_2$ the feature map is $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (1, x_1, x_2, x_1x_2, x_1^2, x_2^2)$. The number of features grows combinatorially in $d$, and the columns become nearly dependent — hence ill-conditioning. Standard remedies: centre and scale $x$ to $[-1,1]$, fit with QR/SVD rather than the normal equations, or regularize (module 05).

2.7 Coefficient of determination

Beyond MSE, regression is often reported with $R^2$: the fraction of variance explained.

$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}}, \qquad SS_{\text{res}} = \sum_i (t_i - \hat{y}_i)^2, \quad SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i (t_i - \bar{t})^2 $$

$R^2 = 1$ means perfect prediction; $R^2 = 0$ means the model is no better than predicting the mean; $R^2 \lt 0$ means it is worse than the mean (which can happen on held-out data). Because $SS_{\text{tot}}$ does not depend on the model, $R^2$ is just a rescaled MSE — but the rescaling makes it comparable across datasets with different target scales.

Lab 1 · the objective

Squared error you can see

Each training point casts a square whose side is the vertical residual — the square's area is that point's contribution to the sum of squares. Move the line with the sliders and watch SSE: the objective is the total area of all the squares.

Try to beat the dashed line by hand. You cannot: it is the least-squares optimum, computed from the normal equations. Notice which squares are red (the line is above the data) and which are green (below) — the optimum balances total area, not the count of errors.

Lab 2 · optimisation

The loss landscape and gradient descent

The plane is parameter space $(w, b)$; the colour is $\log J(w,b)$. It is a single smooth bowl — least squares is convex, so there is no local minimum to get stuck in. Press Run and watch the path bend toward the white ring, which marks the exact analytic optimum.

Now push the learning rate past $0.5$ and run again. The iterates overshoot, then oscillate, then diverge — exactly the $\alpha \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}$ condition derived in §2.5. Drop it to $0.02$ and count how many steps are needed: same destination, very different cost.

Lab 3 · feature maps

Polynomial features, instability and ridge

Degree 3 is roughly the truth. Raise the degree to 9: the training error keeps falling while the amber extrapolation region goes wild and max |w| explodes by orders of magnitude. That is ill-conditioning, not overfitting in the classical sense — the coefficients are being asked to cancel almost-identical columns.

Now add a small ridge penalty $\lambda \approx 0.02$ and watch the fit calm down. This is a preview of module 05: a tiny penalty can turn an unusable model into a serviceable one.

4 Coding from scratch

"""Module 03 — linear regression from scratch: normal equations + gradient descent."""


def transpose(M):
    return [list(row) for row in zip(*M)]


def matmul(A, B):
    Bt = transpose(B)
    return [[sum(a * b for a, b in zip(row, col)) for col in Bt] for row in A]


def matvec(A, v):
    return [sum(a * b for a, b in zip(row, v)) for row in A]


def solve(A, b):
    """Gauss-Jordan elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(A)
    M = [row[:] + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(A)]
    for col in range(n):
        pivot = max(range(col, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][col]))
        if abs(M[pivot][col]) < 1e-12:
            raise ValueError("singular matrix: features are collinear")
        M[col], M[pivot] = M[pivot], M[col]
        pv = M[col][col]
        M[col] = [v / pv for v in M[col]]
        for r in range(n):
            if r != col and M[r][col]:
                f = M[r][col]
                M[r] = [a - f * b_ for a, b_ in zip(M[r], M[col])]
    return [row[-1] for row in M]


def design(X):
    """Prepend a column of ones so the bias is just another weight."""
    return [[1.0] + list(row) for row in X]


def fit_normal_equations(X, t):
    A = design(X)
    At = transpose(A)
    return solve(matmul(At, A), matvec(At, t))       # [b, w1, ..., wm]


def fit_gradient_descent(X, t, lr=0.1, epochs=2000):
    A = design(X)
    n, m = len(A), len(A[0])
    theta = [0.0] * m
    for _ in range(epochs):
        # gradient of (1/2n) * ||A theta - t||^2
        pred = matvec(A, theta)
        resid = [p - ti for p, ti in zip(pred, t)]
        grad = [sum(A[i][j] * resid[i] for i in range(n)) / n for j in range(m)]
        theta = [th - lr * g for th, g in zip(theta, grad)]
    return theta


def mse(t, pred):
    return sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(t, pred)) / len(t)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # y = 3 + 4x with a little noise
    X = [[0.0], [1.0], [2.0], [3.0], [4.0]]
    t = [3.1, 7.2, 10.8, 15.1, 19.0]
    theta_ne = fit_normal_equations(X, t)
    theta_gd = fit_gradient_descent(X, t, lr=0.05, epochs=5000)
    print("normal equations:", [round(v, 4) for v in theta_ne])   # [3.03, 3.99]
    print("gradient descent:", [round(v, 4) for v in theta_gd])

"""Module 03 — the vectorised version: lstsq, pinv, polynomial features, R^2."""
import numpy as np


def design(X, bias=True):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    if X.ndim == 1:
        X = X[:, None]
    return np.hstack([np.ones((len(X), 1)), X]) if bias else X


def fit_lstsq(X, t):
    """Solve min ||A theta - t|| with a QR/SVD-based solver (never invert A^T A)."""
    A = design(X)
    theta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, np.asarray(t, float), rcond=None)
    return theta


def fit_normal_equations(X, t):
    A = design(X)
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ t)      # fine for small, well-conditioned A


def fit_pinv(X, t):
    return np.linalg.pinv(design(X)) @ np.asarray(t, float)


def gradient_descent(X, t, lr=0.1, epochs=1000):
    A = design(X)
    theta = np.zeros(A.shape[1])
    for _ in range(epochs):
        grad = A.T @ (A @ theta - t) / len(A)
        theta -= lr * grad
    return theta


def polynomial_features(x, degree):
    x = np.asarray(x, float).ravel()
    return np.vstack([x**k for k in range(1, degree + 1)]).T


def r2_score(t, pred):
    t, pred = np.asarray(t, float), np.asarray(pred, float)
    ss_res = ((t - pred) ** 2).sum()
    ss_tot = ((t - t.mean()) ** 2).sum()
    return 1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    x = np.linspace(-2, 2, 60)
    t = 0.8 * x**3 - 0.5 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.15, size=x.size)

    Phi = polynomial_features(x, 3)
    theta = fit_lstsq(Phi, t)
    print("coeffs (x, x^2, x^3):", np.round(theta, 3))     # [-0.5, ~0, 0.8]
    print("R^2:", round(r2_score(t, design(Phi) @ theta), 4))

    # conditioning: the normal equations square the condition number
    A = design(polynomial_features(np.linspace(-1, 1, 40), 8))
    print("cond(A)      =", f"{np.linalg.cond(A):.3e}")
    print("cond(A^T A)  =", f"{np.linalg.cond(A.T @ A):.3e}")

"""Module 03 — scikit-learn, the version you ship."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, r2_score
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(-2, 2, 120)
t = 0.8 * x**3 - 0.5 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.15, size=x.size)
X = x[:, None]
X_tr, X_te, t_tr, t_te = train_test_split(X, t, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)

# Plain linear regression
lin = LinearRegression().fit(X_tr, t_tr)
print(f"linear:  w={lin.coef_[0]:.3f} b={lin.intercept_:.3f}  "
      f"test R^2={r2_score(t_te, lin.predict(X_te)):.3f}")

# Polynomial regression = feature map + linear model, wrapped in a pipeline.
poly3 = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree=3, include_bias=False), LinearRegression())
poly3.fit(X_tr, t_tr)
print(f"cubic:   test R^2={r2_score(t_te, poly3.predict(X_te)):.4f}")

# High degree overfits; ridge rescues it. Scale AFTER expanding the basis.
for degree in (3, 9):
    for alpha in (0.0, 1e-3, 1e-1):
        model = make_pipeline(
            PolynomialFeatures(degree=degree, include_bias=False),
            StandardScaler(),
            Ridge(alpha=alpha) if alpha > 0 else LinearRegression(),
        )
        scores = cross_val_score(model, X, t, cv=5, scoring="r2")
        print(f"degree={degree} alpha={alpha:<6} CV R^2 = {scores.mean():.4f}")

# Multiple regression on a real dataset
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
Xd, td = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True)
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LinearRegression()).fit(Xd, td)
print("\ndiabetes R^2:", round(model.score(Xd, td), 3))
print("largest coefficients:",
      sorted(zip(np.round(model[-1].coef_, 1), load_diabetes().feature_names),
             key=lambda p: -abs(p[0]))[:3])
The NumPy block prints the conditioning experiment from §2.3: cond(AᵀA) ≈ cond(A)², which is why lstsq exists. The scikit-learn block shows the pattern you will reuse for every non-linear feature map: expand → scale → regularize → cross-validate.

5 Assessment

Quiz

Q1

The normal equations for least squares are:

Setting $\nabla J = \frac1N A^\top(A\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf{t}) = 0$ gives $A^\top A\boldsymbol\theta = A^\top\mathbf{t}$ — equivalently, the residual $\mathbf{t} - A\boldsymbol\theta$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$.

Q2

Why is the least-squares Hessian $\frac1N A^\top A$ guaranteed to give a global minimum?

A convex function has no spurious local minima. Positive semi-definiteness of the Hessian is exactly convexity for a quadratic. Uniqueness additionally requires full column rank of $A$.

Q3

Squared error is the maximum-likelihood loss when the noise is:

The Gaussian log-density is quadratic in the residual, so maximising the log-likelihood is minimising the sum of squares. Laplace noise gives absolute error; Poisson gives deviance losses.

Q4

Gradient descent on the least-squares cost with Hessian eigenvalues $\lambda_{\max}=4$ and $\lambda_{\min}=0.01$ converges only if:

Each eigendirection needs $|1-\alpha\lambda_i| \lt 1$, so $\alpha \lt 2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.5$. The slowest direction decays like $(1-\alpha\lambda_{\min})^t$, which with $\alpha \approx 2/\lambda_{\max}$ gives a rate set by $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$.

Q5

You fit a degree-9 polynomial on $[-1,1]$ and the coefficients are enormous with wild extrapolation. The most accurate description is:

On $[-1,1]$, $x^8$ and $x^9$ are almost indistinguishable, so their coefficients can be huge and opposite. The condition number of the Vandermonde matrix grows quickly; solve with QR/SVD, rescale the basis, or add a ridge penalty.

Term
Design matrix $A$
Definition
The $N\times(m{+}1)$ matrix of training inputs with a leading column of ones. Row $j$ is sample $j$; the ones column turns the bias into a weight.
Term
Residual
Definition
$r_j = t^{(j)} - \hat{y}^{(j)}$, the signed vertical error. At the least-squares solution the residual vector is orthogonal to the column space of $A$.
Term
Overdetermined system
Definition
More equations than unknowns. Generically no exact solution exists, so we look for the minimum-residual solution instead.
Term
Projection matrix
Definition
$P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ maps any target onto the closest point of $\mathrm{col}(A)$. It is symmetric and idempotent.
Term
Pseudoinverse $A^+$
Definition
$V\Sigma^+U^\top$ from the SVD. It inverts the non-zero singular values, giving the minimum-norm least-squares solution even when $A^\top A$ is singular.
Term
Condition number
Definition
$\kappa = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ of the design matrix. It bounds how much relative error in the data is amplified in the solution, and sets how slow gradient descent will be.
Term
$R^2$
Definition
$1 - SS_{\text{res}}/SS_{\text{tot}}$: the fraction of the target's variance explained by the model. Zero means no better than the mean; negative means worse.
Term
Feature map $\phi$
Definition
A transformation applied to inputs before a linear model. It makes non-linear problems linear in the parameters — the idea behind polynomial regression, kernels, and hidden layers.

Practice problems

Easy

Solve a tiny least-squares problem by hand

Fit $y = wx + b$ to the three points $(0,1), (1,2), (2,4)$ using the normal equations.

$A = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{t}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{bmatrix}$.

$A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}$, $A^\top\mathbf{t} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\11\end{bmatrix}$.

Solving $\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}b\\w\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\11\end{bmatrix}$ gives $w = 1.5$, $b = 2/3 \approx 0.667$. Predictions: $0.667, 2.167, 3.667$; residuals $-0.333, 0.167, -0.333$ — which sum to zero, as they must, because the ones column is in the column space.

Medium

Show that ridge shrinks the solution

For the regularised objective $J_\lambda(\mathbf{w}) = \|A\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{t}\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$, derive the closed-form solution and show that $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\lambda\| \le \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0\|$ in the eigenbasis of $A^\top A$.

  1. $\nabla J_\lambda = 2A^\top(A\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{t}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{w} = 0 \Rightarrow (A^\top A + \lambda I)\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\lambda = A^\top\mathbf{t}$.
  2. Since $A^\top A$ is symmetric PSD, write $A^\top A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ and rotate: $(\Lambda + \lambda I)Q^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\lambda = Q^\top A^\top\mathbf{t}$, so componentwise $\hat{w}_\lambda^{(i)} = \frac{(Q^\top A^\top\mathbf{t})_i}{\lambda_i + \lambda}$ while $\hat{w}_0^{(i)} = \frac{(Q^\top A^\top\mathbf{t})_i}{\lambda_i}$.
  3. Each component is multiplied by the shrinkage factor $\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\lambda} \le 1$, so $|\hat{w}_\lambda^{(i)}| \le |\hat{w}_0^{(i)}|$ and therefore $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\lambda\| \le \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0\|$. ∎
  4. Directions with small $\lambda_i$ (nearly collinear columns) are shrunk the most — which is precisely the ill-conditioned direction that caused the exploding polynomial coefficients.
Hard

Derive the variance of the least-squares estimator

Assume $\mathbf{t} = A\boldsymbol\theta^\* + \boldsymbol\epsilon$ with $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$. Show that $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ is unbiased and that $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) = \sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$. What does this say about collinear features?

  1. $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{t} = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top(A\boldsymbol\theta^\* + \boldsymbol\epsilon) = \boldsymbol\theta^\* + (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\boldsymbol\epsilon$.
  2. $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}] = \boldsymbol\theta^\* + (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\epsilon] = \boldsymbol\theta^\*$: unbiased.
  3. For a linear transform $M\boldsymbol\epsilon$ with independent zero-mean noise of variance $\sigma^2$, the covariance is $\sigma^2 MM^\top$. With $M = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$: $$ \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) = \sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top A (A^\top A)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (A^\top A)^{-1}. $$
  4. ∎ Interpretation. The diagonal entries are the variances of individual coefficients. If two columns are nearly collinear, $A^\top A$ is nearly singular, its inverse has enormous diagonal entries, and the coefficients swing wildly between samples — even though predictions may still be accurate. That is why interpretation and prediction are different problems.

Project 03

A regression you can defend in a meeting

Predict a continuous quantity and, more importantly, explain the model: which features matter, how stable the coefficients are, and where the fit fails.

Brief

  1. Choose a regression dataset with at least 8 features (diabetes, California housing, or a domain dataset you care about).
  2. Implement least squares three ways — normal equations, lstsq, gradient descent — and show they agree to 6 decimals on standardised data.
  3. Report $\kappa(A)$ and $\kappa(A^\top A)$; demonstrate what happens when you drop the standardisation.
  4. Fit polynomial features of degree 1–3 and plot train/validation error. Then add ridge and show the coefficient-norm trajectory as $\lambda$ increases.
  5. Write a residual analysis: residuals vs. fitted, residuals vs. each important feature, and a QQ-style check for normality. State what you conclude about the Gaussian-noise assumption.

Deliverables

  • Notebook with the three implementations compared numerically
  • Three figures: coefficient path, residual diagnostics, learning curves
  • One-page summary written for a domain expert, not an ML reader
